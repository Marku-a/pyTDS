Adaptive TDS: Time Delay Stability that chooses its own parameters

TDS needs a window, a lag range and a tolerance, and nobody knows the right values for a new system. aTDS measures them from the signals. This brief tests each of the three rules against signals whose coupling and delay are known. It does not yet show whether aTDS changes or sharpens sleep-stage contrasts in real sleep networks; that is untested.

The idea

Time Delay Stability (Bashan et al. 2012) links two signals when the delay τ₀ that best aligns them stays nearly constant over consecutive windows. A window counts as stable when at least 4 of 5 neighbouring delays agree within a tolerance. The TDS score is the percentage of stable windows.

The result depends on three numbers: the window, the lag range searched and the tolerance. The published values (60 / ±30 / ±1 samples) were chosen for 1‑Hz sleep recordings. They are counted in samples, so they mean something different for every system and every sampling rate. For a new system they are a guess.

aTDS: you give it the signals, and it sets all three numbers itself.

The 4-of-5 stability rule stays as published. The three rules use only each signal's own structure and coupling-free copies; they never use which pairs are coupled or what the delay is. (A coupled signal's own autocorrelation partly reflects its partner, so B is not entirely blind to coupling.)

How aTDS chooses the three parameters

Window

L = 30 · B

B = 1 + 2 Σ r(k)², summed over the autocorrelation r(k) up to its first zero crossing (the Bartlett factor). For a smooth signal, B neighbouring samples carry about one sample's worth of new information. So L holds about 30 independent samples of the slowest signal in the system. Limits: at least 61 samples (so the lag range is never narrower than the published ±30), at most 10 % of the recording. Optional: if the user knows the longest plausible delay dmax, the window is at least 2·dmax + 1.

Why 30: with n independent samples each correlation value has a random error of about 1/√n (±0.18 at n = 30). 30 is a convention that balances reliability against time resolution; Figure 3b tests it.

Tested in Figures 2, 3 and 3b.

Lag range and step

τmax = (L − 1) / 2

The same ratio as the published setting (±30 in a 60-sample window). Step = L / 2, the published 50 % overlap. If a signal has a rhythm with period P, τmax ≤ P / 2, because beyond half a cycle a delay d and d − P look the same.

Tested in Figure 4.

Tolerance

largest tol with chance + 2 SE ≤ 5 %

One signal is circularly shifted by a large random amount. That keeps each signal's own structure but destroys the coupling. TDS is scored on these shifted copies at tolerance 0, 1, 2, …, and the largest tolerance whose mean chance score, plus two standard errors, stays ≤ 5 % is kept. Rotations are pooled over all recordings available (for a cohort, all subjects). If even 0 exceeds 5 %, the result is flagged calibration_failed.

Tested in Figures 5 and 6.

The algorithm

Input: the signals of one system, s1 … sn, each T samples long. Output: one parameter set used for every pair in the system. The stability rule (4 of 5 windows) is not changed. Constants: 30 independent samples per window, 5 % chance budget, window between 61 samples and 10 % of T, 20 random shifts per pair.

Step 0 · Autocorrelation of each signal

ri(k) = Σt xi(t) xi(t+k) / Σt xi(t)²,   xi = si − mean(si),   k = 0 … T/4

Step 1 · Window

Bi = 1 + 2 Σk=1Ki−1 ri(k)²,   Ki = first k with ri(k) ≤ 0
L = clip( round(30 · maxi Bi), Lmin, 0.1 T ),   step = ⌊L / 2⌋
Lmin = 61,   or max(61, 2·dmax + 1) if the longest plausible delay dmax is given (optional)

The only input is the signals. dmax is optional. Without it, aTDS searches at least ±30 samples, the published range; with it, the lag range is guaranteed to reach dmax (Table 2, fast signal with delay 50).

What B means. Neighbouring samples of a smooth signal nearly repeat each other. Bi (the Bartlett factor) says how many consecutive samples of signal i carry as much new information as one independent sample: B = 1 for white noise, B = 8 means every 8 samples count as one. A window of L samples therefore holds L / Bi independent samples of signal i.

Both signals, one window. B is computed for every signal in the system (for a pair, for x and for y). The largest B, i.e. the slowest signal, sets the window, so every signal, and every pair built from them, has at least 30 independent samples in each window. One window is used for all pairs so that all scores are measured on the same ruler. When signals differ strongly in speed, this gives fast pairs a longer window and a wider tolerance than they need; per-pair windows are the alternative (Table 2).

Why 30. Each value of C(τ) in a window carries a random error of about 1/√n, where n = L / B is the number of independent samples (Bartlett's formula): ±0.18 at n = 30, ±1 at n = 1. But τ₀ is the largest of roughly 30 independent lags, and the largest of 30 pure-noise values is higher, about 0.18 · √(2 ln 30) ≈ 0.47. So a weak coupling (0.3) does not stand out in every single window. What separates it from noise is the 4-of-5 stability rule: noise peaks land at random lags, while the true peak keeps returning to the same lag. More independent samples make τ₀ more reliable but leave fewer windows, and so a coarser picture over time. 30 is a fixed design constant chosen as a balance, not an optimum: with on/off coupling, 45–60 did slightly better (Figure 3b).

Animation: the autocorrelation of x and of y is built one lag at a time and each B is summed up to its first zero crossing (K). The larger B (the slower signal, here x) is used. Then the window is built from independent samples: each dot is one independent sample, worth B samples, until there are 30.

Step 2 · Lag range

τmax = ⌊(L − 1) / 2⌋
if some signal has a rhythm with period Pi:   τmax = min(τmax, ⌊mini Pi / 2⌋)

Rhythm test: after the first zero crossing of ri, take the highest autocorrelation peak; if it is at least 0.3 and not at the edge of the range, its lag is Pi. Otherwise the signal is treated as not rhythmic.

Animation: inside one window, y is shifted against x across the whole lag range while C(k) is drawn; the largest |C| gives τ₀ = −20, the true delay.

Step 3 · Tolerance

1 · Make coupling-free copies. Rotate y in time by a large random amount h (the end wraps around to the start). Each signal keeps its own shape and rhythm, but y no longer follows x, so any stability found between them is pure chance. Repeat with 20 different h. When several recordings of the same kind are available (a cohort), pool the rotated copies of all of them.

2 · Score each copy at tolerance tol. For copy j, find τ₀ in every window (the same window and lag range as above), apply the 4-of-5 rule with this tolerance, and count the stable windows:

scorej(tol) = 100 % × (stable windows of copy j) / (all windows of copy j)

3 · Average over the copies.

C(tol) = meanj scorej(tol)

Because the copies contain no coupling, every stable window in them is a false alarm. C(tol) is therefore the false-alarm rate of TDS at that tolerance: the score you would get from uncoupled signals just by luck.

4 · Choose the tolerance. Try tol = 0, 1, 2, … and keep the largest tol whose upper bound, C(tol) plus two standard errors of that mean, is ≤ 5 %:

tol* = max { tol : C(tol) + 2 · SE(tol) ≤ 5 % },   SE = SDj(scorej) / √(number of copies)

The upper bound is used, not the mean alone, so that the budget also holds on new recordings, not only on the copies used to choose the tolerance. With the mean alone, the chance score on new pairs reached 6.7 %; with the upper bound and pooled rotations it stayed at 3.3–4.4 % (Figure 6).

Why a wider tolerance is wanted at all. A real delay is never perfectly constant; it wobbles from window to window. A wider tolerance lets TDS accept that wobble and see real coupling, but it also lets random τ₀ values agree by luck. The tolerance has to be as wide as possible without letting luck take over.

What the 5 % means. It is a design budget for false stability: on coupling-free copies, at most 5 % of windows on average may be labelled stable. It is not the probability that a link is false. Whether a particular link is significant still needs its own test (its score against its own rotated copies) and, in a network, a correction for testing many links at once. The 5 % is a fixed design constant; a stricter user could use 1 %.

Why stopping at the first tol above 5 % is right. A wider tolerance can only add stable windows, never remove them, so C(tol) only goes up. Once it passes 5 %, every larger tolerance is also above 5 %, and the previous one is the widest tolerance inside the budget. (The upper bound behaves the same way in practice.)

Animation: y is rotated to destroy the coupling (8 rotated copies here; aTDS uses 20). For each tolerance, the middle row shows copy 1: τ₀ in every window, filled where 4 of 5 neighbouring windows agree within the tolerance; that fraction is the copy's score. The bars are the scores of all copies, and their mean is C(tol). The tolerance rises until the upper bound C + 2 SE passes 5 %; the last tolerance at or under 5 % is kept.

aTDS_parameters(s1 … sn):
    # Step 1: window
    for each signal i:
        ri = autocorrelation(si)
        Ki = first k where ri(k) ≤ 0
        Bi = 1 + 2 · sum(ri(k)² for k = 1 … Ki−1)
    Lmin = 61   (or max(61, 2·d_max + 1) if the optional d_max is given)
    L    = clip(round(30 · max(B)), Lmin, 0.1·T)   # Lmin wins if 0.1·T < Lmin
    step = L // 2

    # Step 2: lag range
    lag = (L − 1) // 2
    P   = [dominant_period(ri) for each signal, if rhythmic]
    if P not empty: lag = max(1, min(lag, min(P) // 2))

    # Step 3: tolerance
    null = []
    for each pair (i, j) in the system, in every recording available (at most 50 pairs):
        repeat 20 times:
            h = random integer in [a, T − a),  a = max(L, T // 10)
            null.append( τ₀ series of (si, rotate(sj, h)) with L, step, lag )
    tol = 0
    for t = 0, 1, 2, … up to max(1, lag // 4):
        s  = [ TDS score of each null series, 4-of-5 rule, tolerance t ]
        C  = mean(s);  SE = sd(s) / √len(s)
        if C + 2·SE ≤ 5 %: tol = t
        else: break
    calibration_failed = (C + 2·SE at t = 0) > 5 %

    return window = L, step, max_lag = lag, tolerance = tol

How to read this brief

Everything in the tests below is measured the same way. This section explains each term once, so no figure needs guessing.

Known answer
Most tests use synthetic signals that we generate ourselves. We decide whether a pair is coupled, and with what delay, so we can check whether a method gets it right. One test uses a real EEG pair, where only detection can be checked.
Coupled / uncoupled pair
A coupled pair: y copies part of x with a delay d, y(t) = c · x(t − d) + noise, with coupling strength c between 0 and 1. An uncoupled pair is generated the same way with c = 0, so y has no link to x. Each test scores many coupled and many uncoupled pairs.
TDS score
The percentage of windows labelled stable (4 of 5 neighbouring delays agree within the tolerance). A score well above the chance score of the same data indicates a stable delay, and so coupling. The score alone says nothing about whether the delay is right (Figure 4), and aTDS scores are not comparable with published TDS scores.
Chance score
The TDS score a pair gets when the coupling is destroyed by rotating y in time (its end wraps to its start). It tells how high a score luck alone gives. "Chance" in the tables is the mean over many uncoupled pairs or rotated copies; "95th pct" is the value only 5 % of them exceed.
AUC
How well the TDS score separates coupled from uncoupled pairs: the probability that a randomly chosen coupled pair scores higher than a randomly chosen uncoupled one. 1 = always (perfect), 0.5 = no better than a coin flip. Example: AUC 0.9 means a coupled pair outscores an uncoupled one 9 times out of 10.
Delay found
The share of coupled pairs for which the method reports the right delay: at least 3 stable windows, and the median τ₀ of those windows within 10 % of the true delay (at least ±1 sample).
95 % interval
The range the true value very likely lies in, given the finite number of pairs. For AUC: 1 000 bootstrap resamples (draw the pairs again with replacement, recompute, take the middle 95 %). For shares such as "delay found": the Wilson interval. If two methods' intervals do not overlap, the difference is significant at roughly the 5 % level. For methods scored on the same pairs the proper test is the interval of the paired difference (next entry); overlapping intervals do not mean "no difference".
"Better", "equivalent", "undecided"
For two methods scored on the same pairs, we compute the interval of the difference in AUC. "Better": the 95 % interval of the difference is entirely above 0. "Equivalent": the 90 % interval lies inside ±0.05, i.e. any difference is smaller than 0.05 AUC (an equivalence test, TOST). "Undecided": neither.
Training vs scored pairs
aTDS chooses its parameters on separate training pairs (or, for the EEG, the first half of the record) and is then scored on new pairs it never saw, so the parameters cannot fit the answer by accident. In Table 3, calibration is label-free on the scored network itself (rotations only), as in a real analysis.
Slow-down factor r
One test system made r times slower: memory 2r samples and delay 5r samples. This is what happens when the same physical system is recorded r times faster. At r = 1 the published values fit; the larger r, the worse they fit.

The four settings compared

Published
The fixed TDS setting of Bashan et al. (2012): window 60, step 30, lag ±30 (±29 effective in pyTDS's circular cross-correlation), tolerance ±1, the same for every system.
Rescaled by hand
The published setting multiplied by the slow-down factor r: window 60r, step 30r, lag ±30r, tolerance ±r (at r = 4: 240 / ±120 / ±4). It is what a careful analyst would choose if told how much slower the system is. In real data nobody knows r, so this is a fair but generous baseline.
aTDS
The method of this brief: only the two signals go in (optionally the longest plausible delay dmax; for a network, all of its signals), and it sets window, lag range and tolerance itself.
Oracle
The best of 91 grid settings, chosen by checking the answers on labelled training pairs. It is a practical upper bound, not a method anyone can use, because it needs to know which pairs are coupled; a setting outside the grid could do even better (at r = 16 the oracle reaches only AUC 0.84). How it is computed, for each slow-down factor r:
  1. Generate 20 coupled and 20 uncoupled training pairs, separate from the pairs that are scored later.
  2. Try every combination of 7 windows (30, 60, 120, 240, 480, 960, 1 920 samples; lag range (L − 1)/2 each) and 13 tolerances (0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 12, 18, 27, 40, 60, 90, 135; tolerances larger than the lag range are skipped): up to 91 settings.
  3. For each setting, score the 40 training pairs and compute the AUC.
  4. Keep the setting with the highest training AUC (the first one if several tie).
  5. Score the same 100 + 100 test pairs as the other three settings with it.
How it is shown: grey line in Figure 7; the setting it chose at each r is in the Figure 7 data table. If aTDS is close to the oracle, little is left to gain by tuning; if it is far below, better parameters exist.

Signals used

AR(1)
Smooth random noise whose memory fades exponentially; τc is how many samples it takes to forget its past. The main test signal.
fGn (long memory)
Fractional Gaussian noise: memory that fades slowly, like heart rate or EEG power. Its Hurst exponent H (0.5 = no memory, closer to 1 = long memory) equals the DFA exponent α used in physiology.
AR(2) (rhythm)
A noisy oscillator with a period of about 40 samples, like breathing.
Rulkov neurons
A standard model of two bursting neurons; neuron 0 drives neuron 1 with a delay.
EEG pair
Real sleep EEG δ- and σ-band power of one night at 1 Hz, from the pyTDS repository. The original MATLAB result on it, 43.6 %, is reproduced by pyTDS only with the causal stability anchor (43.5 %); the default anchor, used everywhere else in this brief, gives 58.1 %.

Other terms

B, independent samples
B (Bartlett factor) is how many consecutive samples count as one independent sample. A window of L samples holds L / B independent samples. Bxy is the same for a pair of signals.
Balanced accuracy
Used in Figure 3b: the average of two hit rates. While coupling is on, a window counts as correct only if it is labelled stable and its τ₀ is within ±2 samples of the true delay; while coupling is off, a window counts as correct if it is not labelled stable. 0.5 = guessing, 1 = perfect.
Per-link p-value, FDR
Used in Table 3. A link's p-value is p = (1 + number of its 999 rotated copies that score at least as high as the real pair) / 1 000; the smallest possible value is 0.001. When many links are tested at once, some pass by luck; the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure keeps the expected share of false links among the reported ones (the false-discovery rate) at most 5 %, assuming the links are independent or positively dependent.
v2.1
The current version of the aTDS rules, described in the algorithm section (minimum window 61; tolerance from the upper bound of the chance score).

Verdict

aTDS does automatically what a careful analyst would do by hand: it scales the TDS parameters to the system, without being told the system's time scale.

Against the published setting it is much better whenever the system is slower than that setting assumes. Against the setting rescaled by hand it is equivalent or better where the tests can decide (r = 1, 2, 8), and undecided elsewhere. It does not find the best possible parameters: an oracle that looks at labelled data does better. Its value is automation with a false-alarm budget that held on new data in the main test.

Limits

  • Not optimal, only scaled. With coupling that is always on, longer windows detect better than aTDS (Figures 3 and 7, oracle). 30 independent samples trades some detection for time resolution; for longer coupling episodes, 45–60 did slightly better (Figure 3b).
  • The minimum window sets a minimum lag range. In v2.1 the window is never shorter than 61 samples, so the lag range is never narrower than ±30, the published range. Delays longer than half the window still cannot be found: a fast signal with delay 50 was missed by default (AUC 0.56). The optional longest plausible delay dmax fixes this (AUC 1.00 with dmax = 60). Nothing in the output warns the user when a delay is out of reach: for the fast signal with delay 50 the default score was 4.8 % against a chance score of 4.4 %, which reads as "no coupling". dmax is the only safeguard for long delays in fast signals. dmax was tested only with a correct value (60 for a true delay of 50): an overstated dmax makes the window longer than needed (coarser timing), an understated one leaves the delay out of reach. The minimum of 61 also has a cost: in recordings shorter than about 600 samples, 61 is more than 10 % of the record and takes precedence over the 10 % cap, so there are fewer than 19 windows; and for very fast signals the windows are longer than the 30 · B rule asks, so there are fewer of them.
  • One answer per window, so short events can be missed. aTDS uses longer windows for slow signals (259 samples instead of 60 in Figure 1; 11 minutes on the EEG pair). A maximum window can be set, trading reliability for finer timing. For records whose dynamics change, setting the parameters per segment helped (Table 2).
  • The delay is pinned down only within the tolerance. The calibrated tolerance grows with the time scale (±13 in Figure 1, up to ±56 at r = 16) and can exceed the delay itself. The reported delay was right in Figure 4, but the stability judgement is coarse.
  • 5 % is a budget for the mean, not a significance level. With rotations pooled over several recordings and the upper-bound rule, the mean chance score on new data stayed ≤ 5 % in all rows except one (5.2 %, fast–slow pair with the max-B window). Single recordings still exceed it by luck (95th percentile up to 20 %), so each link needs its own test (Table 3).
  • B depends on the record length for long-memory signals. For H = 0.9, B grew from 13 to 84 as the record grew from 5 000 to 80 000 samples, so the window depends on how long one records.
  • Rhythms and bursts. Delays longer than half a cycle are reported wrongly (as the anti-phase lobe); for Rulkov neurons the delay is reported 3 steps too long. Such signals should first be turned into cycle-by-cycle series.
  • One window for all pairs. Setting the window from the slowest signal keeps all scores on one ruler, but fast pairs then get a wide tolerance (±56 instead of ±3). For pairs whose two signals differ in speed, the pair's joint Bxy gave the same detection with a much shorter window (Table 2).
  • Scores are not comparable with published TDS numbers. Compare an aTDS score with the aTDS chance level of the same data.

Not tested here

  • Coupling that switches with the regime. No test switches the coupling itself on in one regime and off in another (the sleep-stage case); the changing-dynamics test changes only the signals' memory.
  • Sleep stages in real cohorts. Whether parameters should be set per stage or frozen per cohort, and how that changes stage contrasts, needs staged multi-subject recordings, which are not in this repository. The synthetic changing-dynamics test (Table 2) is only a first step.
  • The original surrogate test. Bashan et al. (2012) build the null by mixing signals across subjects within the same sleep stage. That null keeps stage-driven co-modulation, which rotating one signal within a recording removes, so the rotation null can be too lenient on real data. Comparing both nulls needs multi-subject data.

Next steps

  • A public staged sleep cohort: per-stage networks under aTDS and the published setting, with cross-subject, stage-matched surrogates next to the rotation null.
  • Make the pair's joint Bxy the window rule for pairs of different speed, and retest Figures 3–7 with it.
  • Networks with stronger common drivers and chains, to measure how often indirect links appear, and whether partial (conditional) TDS is needed.
  • A regime-switching test in which the coupling itself is on in one regime and off in another, scored per regime (the sleep-stage case).
  • A standard error clustered by recording in the tolerance rule, since rotations of one recording are not independent.
  • A weaker-coupling fast–slow pair (to compare the max-B and Bxy windows on detection, not only on window length), and long-memory signals with stronger coupling.

Methods

Signals. AR(1): x has correlation time τc (φ = e−1/τc), z-scored; the coupled partner is y(t) = c · x(t − d) + √(1 − c²) · η(t), where η is an independent AR(1) process with the same τc; uncoupled pairs use c = 0. Long memory: fractional Gaussian noise (exact circulant-embedding method) with Hurst exponent H. Rhythms: AR(2) with period 40 and pole radius 0.97. Rulkov: two Rulkov maps, neuron 1 driven by neuron 0 through delayed diffusive coupling. Changing dynamics: two AR(1) halves with τc 4 and 16. Real data: the δ and σ power series in pyTDS/data. Records are 20 000 samples long unless stated (30 000 in Figures 1, 2 and 5; 40 000 in Figure 3b; 6 000 for Rulkov; 10 000 in Table 3). Every pair has its own fixed random seed.

aTDS v2.1. As in the algorithm section, with two changes made in this revision: the minimum window is 61 samples (was 20), optionally max(61, 2·dmax + 1), and the tolerance is the largest value for which the mean chance score plus 2 standard errors, over rotations pooled across all available training recordings, stays ≤ 5 % (was: the mean over 20 rotations of one recording). In every figure and table, aTDS sets its parameters on separate training data that are not scored: 5 training pairs in the synthetic tests, the first half of the record for the EEG pair. In Table 3 the calibration is label-free on the scored network itself (rotations only, no knowledge of which links are true), as it would be in a real analysis.

Baselines. Published: window 60, step 30, lag ±30 (±29 effective), tolerance ±1. Rescaled by hand: window 60r, step 30r, lag ±30r, tolerance ±r, using the true slow-down factor r. Oracle: windows 30–1 920 × tolerances 0–135, chosen by AUC on 20 + 20 labelled training pairs.

TDS. pyTDS, circular cross-correlation, z-scored windows, τ₀ = lag of the largest |C(τ)|, stability rule 4 of 5 windows, default (non-causal) stability anchor.

Statistics. AUC: probability that a coupled pair outscores an uncoupled one. Intervals: 1 000 bootstrap resamples of the pairs (paired across methods for differences); Wilson intervals for shares. Equivalence: two one-sided tests, i.e. the 90 % interval of the AUC difference inside ±0.05. Delay found: at least 3 stable windows and their median τ₀ within 10 % (at least ±1 sample) of the true delay. Network links: p = (1 + number of rotations scoring at least the real score) / 1 000, Benjamini–Hochberg at q = 0.05. Rulkov delay: median τ₀ of the stable windows, as for all other rows.

Scope. Synthetic signals with known answers and one real benchmark pair. No staged sleep cohort and no cross-subject surrogates.
