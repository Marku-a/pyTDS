Adaptive TDS: Time Delay Stability that sets its own parameters

TDS needs a window, a lag range and a tolerance, and for a new system nobody knows the right values. aTDS takes only the signals and measures all three from them.

1 · The problem

Time Delay Stability (Bashan et al. 2012) links two signals when the delay τ₀ that best aligns them stays nearly constant from window to window: a window counts as stable when 4 of 5 neighbouring delays agree within a tolerance, and the TDS score is the share of stable windows.

The published setting, window 60, lag ±30, tolerance ±1, was chosen for 1‑Hz sleep recordings and is counted in samples. For a slower system, or the same system recorded faster, the same numbers mean something else, and TDS can miss a coupling that is plainly there.

2 · The idea: three rules

ParameterRuleIn words
WindowL = 30 · BLong enough to hold about 30 independent samples of the slowest signal; at least 61 samples.
Lag range±(L − 1) / 2Grows with the window; capped at half a period if a signal is rhythmic.
Tolerancechance ≤ 5 %The widest tolerance at which coupling-free copies of the data rarely look stable.

The 4-of-5 stability rule is unchanged. The only required input is the signals. Optionally the user can give the longest plausible delay dmax; then the window is at least 2·dmax + 1, so the search reaches that far.

3 · How it works, step by step

Step 1 · Window

Neighbouring samples of a smooth signal nearly repeat each other. From each signal's autocorrelation r(k), the Bartlett factor B = 1 + 2 Σ r(k)² (up to the first zero crossing) says how many samples count as one independent sample. aTDS computes B for every signal, takes the largest (the slowest signal) and sets L = 30 · B, at least 61 samples and at most 10 % of the record.

__A1__

Why 30. The correlation measured in a window with n independent samples has a random error of about 1/√n: ±0.18 at n = 30, ±1 at n = 1. Fewer samples make τ₀ mostly noise; more make it more reliable but leave fewer windows, so less detail over time. In a test where coupling switched on and off, 30 was within 0.015–0.05 of the best window (45–60 independent samples for longer episodes). 30 is a convention that favours time resolution, not an optimum.

Why at least 61. (61 − 1)/2 = 30, so the lag search is never narrower than the published ±30. Without this floor a nearly white signal got a 32-sample window and a ±15 search that could not reach a delay of 20 (AUC 0.47; with the floor 0.59, equal to the published setting). The cost: recordings shorter than about 600 samples get fewer than 19 windows.

Why the slowest signal. One window for all pairs keeps all scores on the same ruler. The price is that fast pairs get longer windows and wider tolerances than they need.

Step 2 · Lag range

In each window, y is shifted against x and the cross-correlation C(k) is computed for every shift k in ±(L − 1)/2; τ₀ is the shift with the largest |C|. Windows move by half their length.

__A2__

Why (L − 1)/2. With the circular cross-correlation TDS uses, a shift by more than half the window cannot be told apart from a shift in the other direction, so (L − 1)/2 is the widest search that is unambiguous. It is also the published ratio (±30 in 60).

Why a step of L/2. The published 50 % overlap, kept unchanged.

Why half a period for rhythms. For a signal that repeats every P samples, a delay d and d − P look the same, so delays are only searched within ±P/2. Within that range the anti-phase lobe can still win, so rhythmic signals are best converted to cycle-by-cycle series (Limits).

Step 3 · Tolerance

y is rotated in time by a large random amount (its end wraps to its start). Each signal keeps its own shape, but the coupling is gone, so any stability left is luck. For each tolerance 0, 1, 2, … aTDS scores 20 such copies per pair, pooled over all recordings available, and averages them into the chance score C(tol), the false-alarm rate at that tolerance. It keeps the widest tolerance whose C(tol), plus two standard errors, stays ≤ 5 %.

__A3__

Why a wider tolerance at all. A real delay wobbles a little from window to window; a tolerance lets TDS accept that wobble. Too wide, and random delays agree by luck.

Why 5 %. A false-alarm budget, not a significance level: on average at most 5 % of windows of uncoupled signals may look stable. It is a design constant; 1 % would be stricter. Whether a link is significant still needs its own rotation test.

Why two standard errors. With the mean alone, the chance score on new pairs reached 6.7 %. With the margin, and rotations pooled over all recordings available, it stayed at 3.3–4.4 %.

4 · How the tests are judged

The tests shown here use synthetic signals whose coupling and delay we set, so each answer can be checked; the full report adds one real EEG pair (detection only). aTDS chooses its parameters on separate training data and is scored on data it has not seen.

  • AUC: the chance that a coupled pair scores higher than an uncoupled one. 1 = perfect, 0.5 = coin flip.
  • Delay found: the share of coupled pairs whose stable windows report the true delay (within 10 %).
  • Slow-down factor r: one test system made r times slower (memory 2r, delay 5r samples), which is what recording it r times faster does. At r = 1 the published values fit.

Four settings are compared:

  • Published: 60 / ±30 / ±1, the same for every system.
  • Rescaled by hand: the published setting multiplied by r (60r / ±30r / ±r). What an analyst would choose if told how much slower the system is. In real data nobody knows r.
  • aTDS: not told r.
  • Oracle: a practical upper bound that no real analysis can use, because it looks at the answers. For each r:
    1. try 91 settings (7 windows from 30 to 1 920 samples × 13 tolerances from 0 to 135);
    2. score 20 coupled and 20 uncoupled labelled training pairs with each;
    3. keep the setting with the highest AUC, and score the same test pairs as the others with it.
    If aTDS is close to the oracle, little is left to gain by tuning.

5 · What the tests show

6 · Verdict

  • Much better than the published setting whenever the system is slower than that setting assumes; equal where it fits.
  • Close to the setting rescaled by hand, without being told r: equivalent at r = 1–2, better at r = 8, undecided at r = 4 and 16, where aTDS is slightly lower.
  • Not optimal. A setting tuned on labelled data does better; aTDS's value is that it needs no tuning.
  • False alarms: the mean chance score on new uncoupled pairs was 3.3–4.4 % in the main test and ≤ 5 % in all other tests but one (5.2 %). Single recordings can exceed it (95th percentile up to 20 %), so each link still needs its own rotation test; with that test and false-discovery control, no false link appeared in 20 uncoupled networks (calibrated without labels on each network).
  • Other signals: better on long memory (H = 0.9), rhythms with a delay below half a period, and signals whose dynamics change halfway; equal on long memory with H = 0.6 and 0.75; detects bursting neurons but reports their delay 3 steps too long.

7 · Limits and open questions

  • Long delays in fast signals are missed silently unless the optional dmax is given (delay 50: AUC 0.56 without it, 1.00 with it). Rhythms with a delay beyond half a period give a wrong delay.
  • Coarser timing: longer windows mean one answer per window, so short coupling episodes can be missed; a maximum window can be set.
  • The delay is known only within the tolerance, which grows for slow signals (up to ±56 here).
  • Scores are on a different scale: aTDS scores are not comparable with published TDS scores; compare each with its own chance level.
  • Window depends on record length for long-memory signals: at H = 0.9, B, and so the window, grew from 13 to 84 as the record grew from 5 000 to 80 000 samples.
  • Untested so far: whether aTDS changes or sharpens sleep-stage contrasts in real sleep networks; the cross-subject, stage-matched surrogates of the original method; coupling that switches on and off with the sleep stage.
