THOUGHT:
The paper proposes aTDS, three data-driven rules for the window length (30 x max Bartlett factor), lag range (half window, capped at half a detected rhythm period) and tolerance (widest t with rotated-copy chance score + 2 SE <= 5 %) of Time Delay Stability. The problem is real: TDS parameters are counted in samples and were fixed for 1-Hz sleep data, and the silent-failure demonstration (high TDS scores with a wrong, edge-of-range delay) is a useful and convincing cautionary result. The paper is also unusually candid: it says itself that full-record cross-correlation/GCC-PHAT beat aTDS on constant-delay data, that rescaling the published setting by an estimated correlation time matches aTDS, that the oracle beats it at every r >= 2, that there is no significant gain on the 35-subject sleep cohort, and that real-data transfer is mixed.

That candour is a strength for scientific integrity, but it also leaves the contribution thin. My main reasoning:
1. Novelty is modest. Scaling a window to the effective sample size (Bartlett/Bayley-Hammersley) and calibrating a threshold on circular-shift surrogates are both standard ideas. Putting them together for TDS is sensible engineering, not a methodological advance.
2. The key claimed advantage is time-resolved detection (regime switching, drifting delay). But the strongest simple competitor, the published setting rescaled by the estimated r (which matches aTDS on the slow-down benchmark), is not evaluated in the regime-switching or drift experiments. In those experiments the published setting is again run on a system slower than its design (tau_c = 8), so "99 % vs 6 %" mostly restates the slow-down result. Without the estimated-rescaling baseline (and a full-record estimator for the drift case, or a sliding full-record estimator), the claim that aTDS adds anything over "rescale by estimated tau_c" is not supported where it matters.
3. The tolerance is calibrated very wide (up to ~10 % of the lag range, +-56 samples at r = 16, +-136 s uncapped in sleep). That changes what "stable" means and makes aTDS scores incomparable with TDS. The paper acknowledges this, but it weakens the interpretability that is TDS's main selling point. False stability in uncoupled regimes (8-9 %) is above the stated budget, and single recordings exceed the budget up to 20 % at the 95th percentile, so the "5 % budget" holds only on average and can be broken by the rotation null's known biases (shared co-modulation, correlated rotations).
4. The real-data evidence is weak. Sleep: no significant gain, and the wake contrast is weaker. Other fields: 2 wins (single records), 1 match, 1 failure (162-day delay), and 1 non-discriminating record. That is n=1 per field with no uncertainty on the delay estimate. In the hourly hydrology and energy cases the aTDS windows (80 d, 27 d) are so long that the result is effectively a full-record estimate, which full-record cross-correlation would also give. These cases do not demonstrate time resolution.
5. Synthetic design. The AR(1) linear-delay model with delay and tau_c scaled together by r favours a time-scale-proportional rule by construction. The sensitivity analysis shows the "30" constant is not well chosen for detection (45-60 is better), so the default is a convention.
6. Smaller points: the non-causal stability anchor gives 58.1 % vs the benchmark's 43.6 % on the reference pair, a large difference that needs explaining since it is the default throughout. The rhythm-detection threshold critically decides whether delays are found (the 0.3 threshold misses d = 30). The Bartlett truncation at the first zero crossing is ad hoc. For long memory, B depends on record length, so the window is not an intrinsic property.
7. The presentation is dense and number-heavy but clear and well organised. Table 7 is helpful. The AI-drafting disclosure and the absence of human expert review are noted. They are not an ethical problem per se, but they reinforce the need for careful verification.

Overall the paper is a careful, honest engineering study with a narrow, partially supported contribution. Its central practical message ("rescale TDS parameters to the time scale, check the lag range, calibrate against rotations") is valuable, but a simple estimated rescaling already achieves most of it, and the distinctive time-resolved advantage is not tested against that baseline. I lean towards rejection at a selective venue, at a score of 5.
