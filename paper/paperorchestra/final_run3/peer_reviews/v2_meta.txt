THOUGHT:
All three reviewers recommend Reject (Overall 4, 5, 4; confidence 4 each). Reviews 1 and 3 are word-for-word identical, so in effect there are two independent opinions, and they agree closely. I weigh them that way and do not count the duplicated view twice.

Points of consensus. The paper deals with a real practical problem: TDS parameters are counted in samples and tied to 1-Hz sleep data. It shows convincingly that a fixed +/-30 lag range can silently give high TDS scores with wrong delays, sometimes with the wrong sign. The evaluation is unusually broad and fair, with an oracle, true and estimated rescaling, full-record XCorr and GCC-PHAT, held-out seeds, sensitivity analyses and equivalence tests. Negative results are reported candidly, and the work is reproducible. All of these are real merits.

The decisive weaknesses are also shared. (1) The method's novelty is low: it combines standard ingredients (effective sample size, the aliasing limit of circular correlation, surrogate calibration) into a parameter heuristic. (2) The default aTDS is statistically indistinguishable from simply rescaling the published setting by an estimated correlation time on every synthetic test. (3) The variant that does beat that baseline, aTDS-BH, was selected on the AR(1) benchmark and validated only on new seeds of the same generator. The paper itself says its gain comes from a longer window, which raising the constant from 30 to 60 independent samples reproduces. It also exceeds the 5% false-stability budget and degrades under regime-limited coupling. (4) Time resolution is the main reason to use TDS over full-record estimators, and the long windows undermine exactly that use case. There is no gain on the motivating 35-subject sleep cohort, and the wake contrast becomes weaker. (5) Positive real-data evidence is thin, with few windows and partly circular reference delays. (6) The calibrated tolerances are wide, which weakens the meaning of "stable delay" and makes scores incomparable with the published TDS literature. This works against the paper's own reproducibility motivation. (7) The rotation null and the SE simplification are anti-conservative. (8) The main results use a non-causal stability anchor that changes scores materially. Reviewer 1/3 also points out an inconsistency between the Limitations section and Table 5.

My own judgement: this is a careful, honest empirical study that would be useful to TDS practitioners as a guidance and benchmarking paper. However, its central methodological claim, that data-driven rules improve on simple rescaling, is not supported for the default method. The improved variant is in effect a tuned window length. Novelty and significance for a top ML venue are limited. Reviewer 2 rates soundness slightly higher (3 vs 2), and I take a middle position: the experiments are sound, but the conclusions drawn for the headline variant are not. Overall 4, Reject. I encourage resubmission that validates the method across generator families, uses the causal anchor, ties the tolerance to the variance of the delay estimate, and compares against sliding-window time-resolved competitors.
