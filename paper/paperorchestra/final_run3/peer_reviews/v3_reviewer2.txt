THOUGHT:
The paper proposes data-driven rules for the three sample-counted parameters of Time Delay Stability (window L, lag range tau_max, tolerance tol): L = 30 x max_i B_i (Bartlett first-zero factor), tau_max = (L-1)/2 capped at half a rhythm period, and tol = widest tolerance whose rotation-surrogate chance score (mean + 2 SE) stays below 5 %. A variant (aTDS-BH) uses a Bayley–Hammersley-type factor that yields roughly twice-longer windows. The evaluation is extensive and unusually candid: synthetic slow-down benchmark, oracle, hand-rescaled and estimated-rescaled baselines, full-record XCorr/GCC-PHAT, long-memory/rhythmic/Rulkov/mixed-speed signals, networks with BH-FDR, regime-switching/nonlinear/drift, a 35-subject sleep cohort, and five non-physiological records.

My main intuitions:
1. Novelty is modest. Each ingredient (effective sample size, circular-correlation aliasing limit, surrogate calibration of a threshold) is standard, and the authors say so. The contribution is engineering a parameter recipe for a specific measure.
2. The paper's own results undercut the headline method. Default aTDS is equivalent to the simplest alternative (published setting rescaled by an estimated correlation time). The authors find "no synthetic test in which it is better with statistical support". Its only edge is one hourly rainfall–runoff record. The better variant, aTDS-BH, was chosen post hoc on the benchmark. Its gain is "essentially that of a longer window", which raising the constant from 30 to 60 matches. So the practical message reduces to "scale TDS to the correlation time, and use longer windows if you can afford them". That is useful but thin.
3. Real-data value in the target domain (1-Hz sleep physiology) is nil: no improvement in stage contrasts, a weaker wake contrast, and an uncapped window (48 min) that cannot resolve stages at all. The non-physiological wins rest on single records with small numbers of windows (e.g. 35/44 stable windows with an 80-day window for an 8-hour delay). The reference delays partly come from cross-correlation itself, which the authors acknowledge.
4. Several technical concerns:
   - The calibrated tolerance grows with the time scale (up to +/-56 samples at r=16, comparable to or exceeding the delay). Stability then becomes coarse and scores are not comparable with published TDS.
   - The "5 % false-stability budget" is a mean-window quantity, not an error rate. It is exceeded in several settings (aTDS-BH 5.2–5.7 %, 8–11 % near regime boundaries, single recordings up to 20 %).
   - The SE in the calibration ignores dependence among rotations and pairs.
   - The Cauchy–Schwarz bound justifies max B for independent series. Applying it after first-zero truncation and to coupled series is heuristic.
   - The main results use a non-causal stability anchor that gives 58.1 % rather than the reference 43.6 % on the benchmark. This is a notable deviation from the original method, and the study was not repeated with the causal anchor.
   - Sliding-window GCC-PHAT, the most natural time-resolved competitor, is not compared.
5. Clarity: the writing is precise and honest but extremely dense. Numbers are piled into the prose, the narrative shifts between three variants, and the key takeaway is buried. The paper reads like a comprehensive technical report rather than a focused contribution.

Overall: a careful, transparent, reproducible study with real practical guidance. But the primary method does not beat a trivial baseline, the better variant is a post-hoc "longer window", and the target-domain application shows no gain. I land around 5 and lean towards Reject for a selective venue; it would fit a software/methods-note venue after condensation.
