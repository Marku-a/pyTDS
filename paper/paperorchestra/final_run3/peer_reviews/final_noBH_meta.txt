THOUGHT:
All three reviewers agree on what the paper is and how good it is. Overall scores are 4, 5 and 5, all three recommend Reject, and every reviewer gives Originality 2, Quality 3, Clarity 2, Significance 2, Soundness 3, Presentation 2, Contribution 2 and Confidence 4. My reading of the reviews matches theirs.

What the paper does well: it targets a real reproducibility problem. TDS parameters are counted in samples and were tuned for 1-Hz sleep data. Each of the three rules has an explicit statistical rationale (Bartlett effective sample size, the aliasing limit of circular correlation, and a surrogate-calibrated false-stability budget). The evaluation is unusually fair: it includes a true-r rescaling, an estimated-r rescaling, an oracle, and full-record cross-correlation and GCC-PHAT, plus a held-out replication and sensitivity analyses. The authors report their negative results candidly. The demonstration that a fixed +/-30 lag range gives high TDS scores with edge-pinned, sometimes sign-flipped delays is a useful cautionary result on its own.

The decisive problem, raised by all three reviewers, is that the paper does not show a benefit over a trivial baseline. The large gains are measured against the published setting used outside its design regime. Against the obvious alternative, rescaling the published setting by an estimated correlation time, aTDS is at best equivalent. That baseline has higher per-link power at r = 4 (0.82 vs 0.66) and lower false stability in the regime-switching test (2.0% vs 8.1%). Reviewers 1 and 2 also note that the rhythm cap made aTDS report the wrong delay in a case the rescaling got right. In the motivating domain, the 35-subject sleep cohort, there is no gain, and the uncapped window cannot resolve sleep stages. The real-data advantage rests on a single hourly rainfall-runoff record whose reference delay is partly circular, and one of the catchments fails.

Several secondary concerns are shared by all reviewers:
- Calibrated tolerances widen to as much as +/-56 samples and can exceed the delay itself. This changes the meaning of "stable" and breaks comparability with published TDS scores, and some of the gain may come from this looser criterion.
- The 5% budget controls only the mean chance score. Single records reach 20%, regime boundaries give 8-9%, and the rotation null ignores shared slow co-modulation, which makes it anti-conservative.
- Truncating the Bartlett sum at the first zero crossing ignores seasonal and negative lobes and makes the window depend on record length for long-memory signals.
- All main results use the non-causal stability anchor, which moves the benchmark score from 43.5% to 58.1%.
- Presentation is dense and repetitive.

The reviewers disagree on one factual point. Reviewer 1 says equivalence to the estimated rescaling is not formally tested on the held-out set. Reviewers 2 and 3 credit the paper with TOST equivalence testing. In either case the per-link power results argue against strict equivalence, so the authors should qualify that claim.

Reviewer 3 raises two further points: the sleep cohort's source and its ethics approval or consent are not documented, and long delays under circular correlation depend on wrap-around overlap. The cohort documentation is a reporting gap that should be fixed, but it does not by itself amount to an ethics concern.

Decision: the work is careful and honest, but its contribution beyond a simple rescaling is not shown, and the original contributions are modest. I recommend Reject with an Overall score of 5, the reviewers' modal score, which credits the rigour and candour. A revision would be much stronger if it found a setting where aTDS beats the estimated rescaling with statistical support, separated the tolerance effect from the window effect, used a stronger null and a causal anchor, and tightened the presentation.
