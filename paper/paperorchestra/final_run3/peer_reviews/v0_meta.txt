THOUGHT:
All three reviewers agree closely (Overall 5, 5, 4; all Reject; confidence 4). They agree that the paper takes on a real reproducibility problem: TDS parameters counted in samples do not carry over across sampling rates and time scales. They also agree that it shows a useful silent failure mode, where a fixed +/-30 lag range gives high TDS scores (72-96%) at wrong, edge-of-range delays, sometimes with the wrong sign. And they praise the evaluation as thorough and unusually candid: oracle, true-r and estimated-r rescaling, full-record cross-correlation and GCC-PHAT baselines, TOST equivalence tests, bootstrap intervals, an ablation of each design constant, BH-FDR for networks, and public code and seeds.

The reviewers also converge on the same central weakness, and I share it. On the main slow-down benchmark a one-line rescaling of the published setting by an estimated correlation time matches aTDS, and full-record estimators beat it on constant delays. The niche the paper claims for aTDS is time-resolved detection under regime switching and drifting delay, but there aTDS is compared only against the unrescaled, mismatched published setting. So the 99% vs 6% result largely restates the slow-down finding, and the added value of the three-rule machinery over the simple heuristic is never shown. All three reviewers ask for this missing baseline. It is the decisive experiment.

Secondary concerns shared by all reviewers:
(i) Calibrated tolerances are wide (up to +/-56 samples, about 10% of the lag range). This coarsens what "stable" means and makes aTDS scores incomparable with published TDS, which works against the reproducibility motivation.
(ii) The 5% false-stability budget is exceeded where it matters most: 8-9% in uncoupled regime-switching segments, and up to 20% at the 95th percentile of single recordings. The rotation null is acknowledged to be anti-conservative under shared co-modulation and correlated rotations.
(iii) Real-data evidence is weak. There is no significant gain on the 35-subject sleep cohort (the home domain), and the wake contrast is weaker. The uncapped window cannot resolve stages, so a manual cap comes back. Outside physiology there are single records with 2 wins, 1 match, 1 failure (the L0123002 catchment, 162-day delay) and 1 non-discriminating case. The wins use 27-80-day windows that are close to full-record estimates.
(iv) Several heuristic choices are influential: the first-zero-crossing Bartlett truncation, the constant 30 (45-60 detect better), the 0.3 rhythm threshold, and the floor and cap. The window also depends on record length for long-memory signals.
Reviewer 2 adds the unexplained 58.1% vs 43.5% gap between the non-causal and causal anchors on the benchmark. Reviewer 3 notes that the main AR(1) model favours a Bartlett-based rule. All three find the presentation dense.

Overall assessment: a careful, honest and reproducible engineering study with a useful warning for practitioners. Its novelty is limited (it combines standard effective-sample-size and surrogate-calibration ideas), and it does not show an advantage over the simplest competitor, either where it claims one or on real data. Soundness of what is reported is good, but contribution and significance are modest. Averaging the reviewers with my own view gives a borderline-reject score of 5 and the decision Reject. I want the meta-review to be constructive: if the authors run the estimated-rescaling and sliding-window GCC-PHAT baselines on the time-resolved and real-data tasks, fix the budget violations, and make the tolerance scale-aware, a resubmission could be competitive.
