THOUGHT:
The paper proposes aTDS, a set of three rules that set the window, lag range and tolerance of Time Delay Stability (TDS) from the data: a window holding about 30 effective samples of the slowest signal (Bartlett first-zero factor, or a Bayley-Hammersley-type variant called aTDS-BH), a lag range of half the window capped at half a rhythm period, and the widest tolerance whose rotation-null chance score stays under a 5 % budget. The evaluation is unusually broad and self-critical: an AR(1) slow-down benchmark, fair baselines (rescaling by the true and by an estimated r, an oracle grid, full-record cross-correlation and GCC-PHAT), held-out validation of the BH variant, a sensitivity analysis, regime-switching, nonlinear and drifting coupling, FDR-controlled networks, a 35-subject sleep cohort, and records from other fields. The limitations are stated openly and in detail.

My main reservations concern how much the method contributes, and to a lesser degree its soundness.
(1) Novelty is modest. Every ingredient is standard (effective sample size, the aliasing limit, rotation surrogates), and the authors say so. The decisive question is whether the rules beat a simple heuristic. By the paper's own account, the default aTDS does not beat the published setting rescaled by an estimated correlation time on any synthetic test with statistical support. Its only edge is the 8-h delay on a single hydrology record.
(2) The variant that does beat the rescaling, aTDS-BH, was selected on the benchmark it is reported on. It is validated on held-out data, but only with new seeds of the same AR(1) generator, which is weak out-of-distribution evidence. The paper itself concedes that the gain is "essentially that of a longer window": raising the constant 30 to 60 gives nearly the same AUC. So the result reduces to a choice of the effective-sample constant, which also trades off against time resolution, the very property that motivates TDS. aTDS-BH also exceeds the false-stability budget (5.2-5.8 %) and fails in the regime-switching test.
(3) In the motivating domain (1-Hz sleep physiology) there is no gain. The uncapped window cannot resolve sleep stages, and the wake contrast weakens. The positive real-data cases are outside physiology: a few hourly or half-hourly records with long windows (80 days, 44 windows), where a full-record cross-correlation would do as well. The reference delays there are partly defined by that same cross-correlation.
(4) Calibrated tolerances are wide (about 10 % of the lag range, up to ±56 samples, and ±1.8 days when the claimed delay is 8 h). "Stability" at this tolerance is a much weaker statement than in the original TDS, and the scores become non-comparable with the literature. The paper acknowledges this, but it weakens the interpretive appeal of TDS.
(5) Some methodological points remain open. The rotation null ignores shared slow co-modulation. The SE treats correlated rotations as independent. The main results use a non-causal stability anchor that inflates the benchmark score (58.1 vs 43.5 %), and causal-anchor replication is listed as future work. Circular correlation with τmax = (L-1)/2 makes overlap shrink at long lags. The 4-of-5 rule is kept without question.
(6) Clarity is good but dense; the paper reads like an exhaustive technical report, with many numbers in the running text. It is single-author, LLM-drafted, and declared not yet reviewed by a human expert. That is disclosed transparently and is not itself a reason to reject.

Overall: a careful, honest and reproducible engineering study with useful practical guidance for TDS users. The core scientific contribution is incremental, though, and the main claimed advantage (aTDS-BH) amounts largely to "use longer windows". I put it at 5, leaning reject for a general venue. It could suit a methods or software journal in network physiology.
