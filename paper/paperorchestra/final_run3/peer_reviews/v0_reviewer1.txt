THOUGHT:
The paper proposes aTDS, three data-driven rules for setting the window, lag range and tolerance of Time Delay Stability. The window holds 30 Bartlett-independent samples of the slowest signal. The lag range is (L-1)/2, capped at half a detected rhythm period. The tolerance is the widest one whose chance score on circularly rotated copies (mean + 2 SE) stays at or below 5%. The motivation is sound and practical: TDS parameters are counted in samples, so they silently lose meaning when the sampling rate or time scale changes. The lag-range demonstration (high scores with wrong, edge-of-range delays, some with the wrong sign) is a useful cautionary result for the network-physiology community.

The manuscript is unusually honest and self-limiting. It concedes that full-record cross-correlation and GCC-PHAT beat aTDS on stationary data, that the published setting rescaled by an estimated correlation time matches aTDS, that the oracle beats it at every r >= 2, and that on the 35-subject sleep cohort (its home domain) it brings no significant gain and weakens the wake contrast. That candour is commendable, but it also shrinks the contribution a lot. What remains is a reasonable heuristic recipe whose main claimed edge over a trivial alternative (rescale by an estimated tau_c) rests on time-resolved cases, regime switching and drifting delay. Those cases were compared only against the unscaled published setting, not against the estimated rescaling. That is the key methodological gap: the paper's own stated selling point is not tested against the strongest simple baseline. The real-data wins (hourly rainfall, temperature-demand) are likewise compared only against the clearly mismatched published setting, not against the estimated rescaling. One of three catchments fails outright, and the oil-gasoline record is uninformative.

Other technical concerns:
(1) The calibrated tolerance grows to about 10% of the lag range and can exceed the delay itself, so "stability" becomes coarse and the meaning of the score drifts. Scores are explicitly not comparable to published TDS.
(2) The rotation null ignores shared slow co-modulation and treats correlated rotations as independent in the SE. Both biases favour wider tolerances, and the false-stability budget is violated in the regime-switching test (8-9%) and in single recordings (95th percentile up to 20%).
(3) The Bartlett factor is truncated at the first zero crossing and depends on record length for long-memory signals, so the "intrinsic" window is not intrinsic.
(4) The constant 30 is admittedly a convention; detection improves monotonically with it, so the rule mainly trades time resolution, and the paper does not offer a principled way to choose it.
(5) The synthetic evaluation relies heavily on linear AR(1) coupling, where cross-correlation is ideal.
(6) The network FDR result is imprecise (10 networks; FDP 8.3%).

Clarity: well organised, with good notation and summary tables, but very dense and number-heavy. Many hedges make the central message diffuse. Originality is moderate: effective sample size and surrogate calibration are standard tools, applied here to TDS parameter choice. Significance is limited to TDS users in non-1-Hz settings.

Overall: careful, reproducible, honest engineering work with modest novelty and an unproven advantage over the simplest alternative. Borderline; I lean toward rejection in its current form. A version that benchmarks estimated rescaling on the time-resolved and real-data tasks could be acceptable.
