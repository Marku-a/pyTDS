THOUGHT:
The paper proposes aTDS, three data-driven rules for the window, lag range and tolerance of Time Delay Stability: (1) a window holding 30 Bartlett-effective samples of the slowest signal, (2) a lag range of (L-1)/2 capped at half a detected rhythm period, and (3) the widest tolerance whose rotation-null chance score, as mean + 2 SE, stays at or below 5 %. The problem is real. TDS parameters are counted in samples and are routinely carried over to data with other time scales and sampling rates. The failure the paper shows in Fig. 4 is a useful warning that practitioners should hear: a fixed lag range can give high TDS scores together with a wrong delay, sometimes with the wrong sign.

My main reservations are about how much the method adds, not about how honestly it is reported. The authors' own stronger baselines undercut the method's value:
- A simple rescaling of the published setting by an estimated correlation time is "equivalent" on synthetic data. It has more per-link power at r = 4 (0.82 vs 0.66). It shows less false stability in the regime-switching test.
- Full-record cross-correlation and GCC-PHAT dominate on stationary, constant-delay data.
- On the 35-subject sleep cohort, the very application TDS was built for, no adaptive setting improves stage contrasts, and the wake contrast becomes weaker.
- The only case where aTDS beats the estimated rescaling is a single hourly rainfall–runoff record. Its reference delay comes partly from the full-record cross-correlation, which the authors themselves call circular.
- On one of three catchments aTDS fails outright, because the first-zero Bartlett truncation ignores the seasonal lobe.

So the contribution amounts to a sensible, well-documented heuristic with a stated false-stability budget. It is not shown to be better than the obvious alternative, and the individual ingredients (Bartlett effective sample size, the circular-correlation aliasing limit, rotation surrogates, BH-FDR) are all standard.

Technical points:
- Widening the tolerance (up to ±56 samples, about 10 % of the search range) changes what "stable" means. aTDS scores cannot be compared with published TDS scores, and the tolerance can exceed the delay itself. This substantially weakens the interpretability that is TDS's main appeal.
- The circular correlation with τmax = (L-1)/2 means that at long delays most of the window is wrap-around. Scores fall at d = 160 (27–62 %). A zero-padded linear correlation would avoid this.
- The rotation null ignores shared slow co-modulation, and the SE treats correlated rotations as independent. Both bias toward wider tolerances; the authors acknowledge this.
- False stability near regime boundaries (8–9 %) exceeds the budget unless boundaries are known.
- The synthetic training pairs come from the same generator as the test pairs, which favours calibration.
- The main results use the non-causal stability anchor, yet that anchor changes the benchmark score from 43.5 % to 58.1 %. This is a large effect that is not propagated through the study.
- The Cauchy–Schwarz bound argument for max_i B_i is correct. However, both the window and the tolerance then depend on the slowest signal, which costs fast pairs precision (±56 vs ±3), and the joint B_xy alternative is tested in only one configuration.

Presentation: the paper is unusually candid and quantitative, and every claim is scoped. But it is extremely dense, number-heavy and repetitive: the practical-guidance bullets duplicate each other (window length, and "more independent samples"), and many results are restated in the text, Table 8 and the Conclusion. The narrative reads as an accumulation of follow-up experiments rather than a focused argument. Terminology such as "real pair" for the unrotated synthetic pair (Fig. 5b) is confusing. The sleep cohort's provenance (an MSc project) comes with no description of ethics approval or consent. The manuscript also states that it was AI-drafted and has not been reviewed by a human expert.

Overall, the work is solid and honest, but its significance is limited: the method does not beat a simple estimated rescaling, does not help in the target physiological application, and its real-data advantage rests on a single record. I lean toward reject, scoring 5. This is a reasonable software or methods note; for a stronger venue it would need a clearer demonstrated advantage, for example on real time-varying-delay benchmarks or mixed-speed physiological networks.
