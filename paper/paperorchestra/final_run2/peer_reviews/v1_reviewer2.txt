THOUGHT:
The paper proposes aTDS, three data-driven rules for choosing the window length, lag range and tolerance of Time Delay Stability (TDS). Window = 30 x max Bartlett factor; lag range = (L-1)/2 capped at half the period of any detected rhythm; tolerance = the widest value whose rotation-null chance score (mean + 2 SE) stays at or below 5 %. The problem is real and practically relevant. TDS parameters are counted in samples, and the published 60/±30/±1 values are carried over to data where they do not fit. The paper is unusually honest and self-critical. It reports equivalence tests, bootstrap intervals, an oracle upper bound and negative results on real sleep data, and it lists many of its own limitations.

My main reservations concern novelty and the strength of the evidence for the claimed practical value.
(1) Each component is standard: effective sample size (Bartlett), the Nyquist-like aliasing limit of the circular correlation, and surrogate calibration. The authors acknowledge this. The contribution is engineering and evaluation, not a methodological idea.
(2) The synthetic evaluation is dominated by linear AR(1) pairs with always-on coupling. The "slow-down" test is effectively the same system oversampled by r. An obvious simple baseline is missing: downsample to the published time scale, or estimate the correlation time and rescale the published setting by it, i.e. a one-parameter version of the "rescaled" baseline driven by an estimated r. aTDS is only equivalent or undecided against the hand-rescaled setting at most r, and the oracle beats it at every r ≥ 2. The window rule is admittedly a compromise rather than an optimum, and the tolerance rule's AUC gain is not significant.
(3) The physiological application, which motivates the whole paper, does not show a benefit. Uncapped, the window (48 min) is useless for staging. Capped at 300 s (a user choice that removes the "parameter-free" claim), aTDS reproduces the LS–DS contrast without gain and weakens the wake contrast. The positive real-data evidence comes from two non-physiological records (rainfall–runoff, temperature–demand), with one record each and windows of 27–80 days.
(4) Statistical concerns: the rotation null removes shared slow co-modulation, and its SE treats correlated rotations as independent. Both bias the tolerance toward being too wide, which the authors acknowledge. Calibrated tolerances of ±13 to ±56 samples make "stability" coarse, sometimes wider than the delay itself. The 4-of-5 rule with a tolerance of about 10 % of the lag range is a weaker criterion than the original, so scores are not comparable with the literature.
(5) Some internal inconsistencies. "Delay found" uses the median τ0, while the reported delay uses the mode. The non-causal anchor gives 58.1 % against 43.5 % on the benchmark, yet the paper uses the non-causal anchor everywhere, which deviates from the reference implementation and deserves justification. "Train/test separation" uses training pairs from the same generator, which is weak separation. Many design constants (5 %, 61, 2 SE, 0.3 rhythm threshold, first-zero truncation) were not varied. No comparison with standard delay estimators (GCC-PHAT, coherence-based).
(6) Presentation is dense but clear. The abstract is overloaded with numbers. There is an AI-drafting disclosure, and the paper states that no human expert has reviewed it.

Overall: a careful, honest, useful technical note or software-methods contribution with modest novelty. Its main motivating application (sleep networks) shows no benefit. This is borderline; I lean toward reject for a selective venue. A journal such as SoftwareX or Frontiers in Network Physiology (methods) might be a better fit after the missing baselines are added.
