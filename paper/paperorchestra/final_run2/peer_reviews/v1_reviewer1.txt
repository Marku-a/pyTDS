THOUGHT:
The paper proposes aTDS, a set of three data-driven rules for the parameters of Time Delay Stability (TDS): a window holding ~30 Bartlett-effective samples of the slowest signal, a lag range of half the window (capped at half a detected rhythm period), and a tolerance chosen as the widest value whose rotation-null chance score (mean + 2 SE) stays below 5 %. The paper is unusually candid and carefully scoped. It reports negative and mixed results openly (the oracle wins at every r >= 2, the sleep cohort shows no gain and a weaker wake contrast, the mean-TDS REM change is not counted as an improvement), and its limitations section is thorough.

My main concerns are novelty and how much the evidence shows. Each ingredient is textbook: Bartlett effective sample size, the circular-correlation aliasing bound, and circular-shift surrogates. The authors say so themselves, and the contribution is their combination. That is useful engineering for TDS users, but it is a modest methodological advance.

The synthetic evaluation is built mostly on AR(1) signals with linear, constant-delay coupling and an AR(1) partner noise. This is close to the setting the window rule assumes, so the rule's success there is somewhat circular. The slow-down protocol scales tau_c and d together by r. A rule that scales parameters with the autocorrelation time will naturally track that by construction. The strongest single result (lag range, Fig. 4) is almost trivial: a fixed +/-30 search cannot find a delay of 160. It is still a fair warning to practitioners.

Against the fair baseline (published setting rescaled by the true r), aTDS is equivalent at r = 1–2, better only at r = 8 by a small margin, and undecided at r = 4 and 16. The labelled oracle is clearly better, mainly through longer windows with tol = 0. This suggests the 30-sample convention and tolerance widening are not the most effective way to use the data when time resolution is not needed.

The tolerance widening changes what "stable" means. Tolerances reach 5–10 % of the lag range and can exceed the delay itself, so aTDS scores are not comparable with published TDS. That undercuts the reproducibility motivation. The rotation null is known to be anti-conservative when slow shared co-modulation exists, and the SE ignores clustering. The authors acknowledge both, but both push towards wider tolerances, and neither was corrected.

In the motivating application domain (sleep network physiology), the method needs a manual cap. The uncapped rule gives a 48-minute window that cannot resolve sleep stages, and once capped it brings no significant gain while weakening the wake contrast. The cross-field records (rainfall–runoff, temperature–demand) are positive but anecdotal: n = 1 each, with known delays established partly from the full-record cross-correlation. With 80-day and 27-day windows, the comparison mainly shows that a much longer window helps for stationary constant delays. That is again unsurprising.

There is also no comparison with standard delay estimators (GCC/PHAT, coherence-based), and several design constants were never varied.

Presentation is dense but clear, with good notation, a summary table and practical guidance. Some sections read as an exhaustive list of numbers, and the paper is long relative to its core idea. The network FDR experiment is small (10 networks per design).

Overall: honest and technically competent, but incremental. Its gains appear mostly in regimes that the design rules effectively assume, and its benefit for the main real application is not demonstrated. I lean towards rejection (5), while recognising its value as a practical software and methods note.
