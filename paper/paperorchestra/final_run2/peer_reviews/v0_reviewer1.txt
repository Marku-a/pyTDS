THOUGHT:
The paper proposes aTDS, three data-driven rules for the TDS parameters: a window of about 30 Bartlett-effective samples of the slowest signal, a lag range of half the window capped at half the shortest detected period, and a tolerance chosen as the widest value whose rotation-null chance score (mean + 2 SE) stays at or below 5 %. The problem is real. TDS parameters are fixed in samples and tuned for 1 Hz sleep data, and reproducibility across implementations is a known issue. The rules are reasonable and grounded in classical statistics (Bartlett/Bayley-Hammersley effective sample size, the circular-correlation aliasing limit, surrogate calibration). Methodologically, none of these is a deep novelty: each is a standard heuristic. The contribution is in putting them together and, above all, in the careful evaluation.

The evaluation is the strongest part. It isolates each rule, compares against fair baselines (published, rescaled by hand with the true r, and a labelled oracle), uses paired bootstrap AUC with TOST equivalence, reports Wilson intervals, and has a train/test split for calibration. The lag-range finding is important and practically useful for the field: a fixed ±30 lag gives high TDS scores at a wrong edge delay, with the wrong sign in 16 of 50 cases. The network FDR test and the out-of-field records (rainfall-runoff, temperature-demand) add breadth. The paper is unusually honest. The oracle beats aTDS at every r ≥ 2, aTDS is undecided against the rescaled setting at r = 4 and 16, the sleep cohort shows no significant gain and a loss of wake contrast, and the uncapped window is unusable for staging.

Concerns:
(1) Tolerance widening dilutes the meaning of "stable delay". The tolerance reaches ±56 at r = 16 and ±136 s in the sleep cohort, and can exceed the delay itself. The chance score is controlled, but what TDS measures changes. Stability becomes coarse, and scores are not comparable to published TDS (the authors acknowledge this).
(2) The rotation null ignores shared slow co-modulation and treats correlated rotations as independent. Both biases favour a wider tolerance. This is acknowledged but not fixed, and the 95th percentile of single-recording chance scores reaches 20 %.
(3) The window rule is justified by "30 independent samples", but the oracle results show that this is not detection-optimal. The real criterion is a time-resolution trade-off that the rule does not encode, and the sleep application needed an ad hoc 300 s cap. This undermines the "signals choose the parameters" framing for the main target domain.
(4) The Bartlett factor uses truncation at the first zero crossing and max over signals. For long-memory signals B depends on record length, so the window is not intrinsic.
(5) The synthetic signals are mostly linear AR(1) with additive coupling. Physiological coupling is nonstationary, and the only real physiological validation (sleep) is mixed, with no ground truth.
(6) The paper compares only against TDS variants, not against generic delay-estimation approaches (e.g. GCC/PHAT, or windowed coherence-based delay).
(7) Minor points: reference [12] is cited "In arXiv" for a 1946 paper; Table 6 is split across pages; the paper is dense; the version labelling ("v2.1", "an earlier rule") leaks development history. The AI-drafting disclosure and the statement that no human expert has reviewed it are noted. They are not an ethical problem per se, but the author should verify everything carefully.

Overall: a technically careful, well-scoped, moderately original methods paper with practical value for the network-physiology community, but limited conceptual novelty and an inconclusive gain on the main physiological application. Borderline. I lean slightly positive (6) because the claims are well supported and honestly scoped.
