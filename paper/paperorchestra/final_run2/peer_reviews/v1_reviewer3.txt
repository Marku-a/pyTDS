THOUGHT:
The paper proposes aTDS, three data-driven rules for the window length, lag range and tolerance of Time Delay Stability (TDS). The window holds about 30 Bartlett-effective samples of the slowest signal. The lag range is half the window, capped at half a detected period. The tolerance is the widest one whose rotation-null chance score, plus 2 SE, stays within a 5 % budget. The motivation is real. TDS parameters are counted in samples and the published 60/±30/±1 setting is tied to 1 Hz sleep data. The silent-failure demonstration in Fig. 4, where a fixed ±30 lag range gives high scores at a wrong edge delay, is useful to practitioners.

Originality: each component is standard (effective sample size, the aliasing limit of circular correlation, rotation surrogates), and the authors say so. The contribution is putting them together and evaluating the result. That is modest, mainly an engineering and recommended-practice contribution for one niche coupling measure.

Evaluation quality: careful and unusually candid. It has bootstrap CIs, TOST equivalence, train/test separation for calibration, an oracle upper bound, and many signal classes. I have several concerns.
(1) The main slow-down benchmark sets tau_c = 2r and d = 5r. The delay scales with the memory by construction, which is exactly the assumption the window and lag rules encode. Generalisation to systems where the delay and the correlation time are decoupled (long delay, short memory) is only probed in the fast-signal/dmax case, and there aTDS fails without user input and gives no warning.
(2) Against the fair baseline (rescaled by hand), aTDS is equivalent or undecided at 4 of 5 r values and better only at r = 8. Against the oracle it is clearly worse. So the gain is mostly automation, not detection.
(3) The calibrated tolerance can be large (±56, larger than some true delays). A "stable" label then means stability to about a tenth of the lag range. This changes what TDS measures, and the scores are no longer comparable to the literature. The authors acknowledge this, but it weakens the "same method, better parameters" framing.
(4) The real physiological application, which is the stated motivation (network physiology), is essentially negative. The uncapped window cannot resolve sleep stages. Capped at a user-chosen 300 s, the method reproduces the LS–DS contrast with no gain and weakens the wake contrast. The cap reintroduces a manual parameter, which undercuts the "parameter-free" claim.
(5) The rotation null ignores shared slow co-modulation and treats correlated rotations as independent. Both effects bias toward a wider tolerance, and on real staged data this is untested. The 5 % budget holds only on average, and single recordings reach about 20 %.
(6) There is no comparison with generic delay estimators (GCC-PHAT, coherence-based methods) or with simple full-record cross-correlation. On the stationary synthetic data and the hydrology/energy records those would probably recover the delays trivially. That leaves open whether TDS with aTDS adds anything for those use cases.
(7) The real-data successes in other fields involve 27–80 day windows, and the "known" delays are partly defined by the full-record cross-correlation peak, which is somewhat circular.
(8) Design constants (5 % budget, floor of 61, 2 SE, 0.3 rhythm threshold, first-zero truncation) were not varied. The network FDR test has only 10 networks per design.

Presentation is dense but clear, with honest scoping and a good summary table. Some sentences are overloaded with numbers. The benchmark discrepancy (43.5 % causal vs 58.1 % default non-causal anchor) deserves more explanation, since the default differs from the original method. The disclosure that the paper was LLM-drafted and not reviewed by a human expert is noted; it is not an ethical violation in itself.

Overall: sound and honest, but incremental. The headline real-data application does not show a benefit, and the evaluation design partly builds in the method's assumptions. I lean toward rejection at a selective venue (5). It would be suitable as a methods note or software paper after revision.
