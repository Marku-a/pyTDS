THOUGHT:
The paper proposes aTDS, a set of three data-driven heuristics for choosing the window length, lag range and tolerance of Time Delay Stability (TDS). The window is 30 times the largest Bartlett inflation factor, the lag range is half the window (capped by half a rhythm period), and the tolerance is the widest value whose chance score on circularly rotated copies stays under a 5% false-stability budget. The problem is real: TDS parameters are counted in samples, so their physical meaning depends on sampling rate and time scale, and the field uses them without justification. The paper is unusually careful and self-critical. It reports equivalence tests, paired bootstrap intervals, an oracle upper bound, a hand-rescaled baseline, and negative or mixed results on real sleep data. The lag-range result is the clearest and most useful finding: with a fixed lag range, a delay outside the range gives high TDS scores with a wrong delay. This is a valuable cautionary result for practitioners.

My concerns are about how much the paper contributes and how far the evidence supports the method as an improvement:
(1) Novelty is modest. Each rule is a standard tool applied to TDS: effective sample size via Bartlett/Bayley–Hammersley, the aliasing limit of a circular correlation, and a surrogate-calibrated threshold. The combination is sensible engineering rather than a methodological advance.
(2) The key synthetic comparison (slow-down test) is built so that the published setting fails by construction: tau_c and d scale with r while the parameters stay fixed. The fair comparison is against the hand-rescaled setting. There aTDS is only equivalent (r = 1, 2), better at one r (8, with an interval barely above 0), and undecided at r = 4 and 16. A missing and obvious baseline is a rescaling by an estimated time scale, for example the published setting multiplied by an estimated tau_c or by B/B_ref. The window rule is essentially this, so the separate value of the tolerance calibration and the lag rule beyond simple rescaling is unclear. The tolerance-only ablation shows no significant AUC gain.
(3) The oracle is better at every r >= 2, and it picks long windows with tolerance 0. aTDS instead picks windows near the time-resolution compromise and inflates the tolerance (up to ±56 samples, which can exceed the delay itself). This raises a conceptual issue. With very wide tolerances, the "stability" in TDS no longer means a stable delay in the original sense, and scores are not comparable with the literature. The authors acknowledge this, but it weakens the claim that aTDS preserves TDS's interpretability.
(4) Synthetic signals are mostly linear AR(1) with linear lagged coupling. This is the ideal case for cross-correlation delay estimation and does not reflect the non-stationary, state-dependent coupling that TDS targets in physiology. The on/off test is helpful but limited.
(5) The real physiological evidence is weak and mixed. The uncapped window is unusable for sleep staging, which is the method's home application. Capped at 300 s (an ad hoc user choice that partially defeats the "signals choose" premise), aTDS shows no significant gain on LS–DS, a significantly weaker W–DS contrast for significant links, and removes a REM<DS inversion in mean TDS. Whether that change is "correct" cannot be judged without ground truth. The tolerance is still calibrated with a rotation null that the authors themselves argue is too lenient on staged data.
(6) The cross-field examples are anecdotal: n = 1 per field, and the "known" delays partly come from the full-record cross-correlation peak, which is circular as validation. The published setting is also a straw man there, for example 60 samples on hourly hydrology.
(7) Statistical details. The tolerance SE assumes independent rotations (acknowledged). Single-recording chance scores reach 20% at the 95th percentile. The network test with 10 networks per design cannot establish FDR control. Several design constants (5% budget, 61 floor, 2-SE margin, 20 rotations) are not varied.
(8) Disclosure: the manuscript was drafted by an LLM pipeline and has not been reviewed by a human expert. This is not an ethical violation, since it is disclosed, but it is a concern about validation. The writing is dense, and many numbers are given inline in ways that hinder readability, although overall clarity is reasonable.

Overall, this is a careful, honest and reproducible methods note with useful practical guidance, especially on the lag range. However, the improvement over a simple rescaling is not established, the target physiological application shows no clear gain, and the novelty is incremental. I lean toward reject at a selective venue. It would fit a software/methods journal or a workshop.
