THOUGHT:
All three reviewers agree closely: each gives Overall 5, Confidence 4, Originality 2, Quality 3, Clarity 3, Significance 2, Soundness 3, Presentation 3, Contribution 2, and each recommends Reject. I agree with that verdict after weighing the strengths they share against the weaknesses they share.

What the paper does well, according to all three: it targets a real reproducibility problem. TDS parameters are counted in samples, and the published 60/±30/±1 setting gets reused on data with other time scales. Each rule has a clear, label-free rationale (Bartlett effective sample size, the aliasing limit of circular correlation, a surrogate-calibrated false-stability budget). The silent wrong-delay failure is a convincing finding: with a fixed ±30 lag range, all 50 pairs score 72–96% at an edge delay, 16 of them with the wrong sign. The evaluation is rigorous (paired bootstrap AUC, TOST, Wilson intervals, a hand-rescaled baseline and an oracle bound), and the paper reports its negative results unusually honestly. An inclusive chair should credit all of this.

What decides it, also raised by all three:
(1) The novelty is a combination of standard parts.
(2) Against the fair baseline (rescaled by hand with the true r), aTDS is equivalent or undecided except at r = 8, and the oracle beats it at every r >= 2. So the gain is mostly automation, not better detection. The synthetic benchmark scales the delay with the correlation time, which matches the window and lag rules by construction.
(3) The motivating application gives no benefit. In the sleep cohort, the uncapped 48-min window cannot resolve stages. The 300-s cap is a manual choice that undercuts the parameter-free claim. Even with the cap, there is no significant gain and the wake contrast becomes significantly weaker.
(4) The calibrated tolerances are wide (up to ±56 samples, sometimes larger than the delay). This changes what "stable" means and makes scores incomparable with the published TDS literature, which works against the reproducibility motivation.
(5) The rotation null is lenient: it removes shared slow co-modulation, and the SE treats correlated rotations as independent. Single recordings reach about 20% chance score against a 5% budget.
(6) There are no simple baselines: estimated-time-scale rescaling, downsampling, GCC-PHAT, or full-record cross-correlation.
(7) The design constants are never varied, and the network FDR experiment is small and inconclusive (FDP 8.3%).
Reviewers 2 and 3 also note that the default non-causal anchor departs from the original method (58.1% vs 43.6% on the benchmark) without enough justification. All reviewers note that the manuscript was drafted with LLM assistance and has not been checked by a human expert, so the numbers need independent verification. This does not raise an ethical concern.

My view: this is a careful and useful engineering contribution, and it could be a good fit for a methods journal or a workshop. However, the evidence of practical gain over a simple rescaling, and in the target domain, is too weak for a top-tier venue. Paths to acceptance would include: an estimated-rescaling baseline, a stage-matched or clustered null for calibrating the tolerance, a decoupled delay-versus-memory benchmark, nonlinear or regime-switching coupling, an automatic out-of-range warning, and sensitivity analyses. Following the consensus: Overall 5, Reject, Confidence 4.
