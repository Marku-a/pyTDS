Adaptive Time Delay Stability: what changes when TDS picks its own window and tolerance

Asaf Markuza · analysis assisted by Claude (Anthropic) · code: Marku-a/pyTDS, branch claude/new-session-1at9bf
Abstract

Time Delay Stability (TDS) links two physiological signals when the delay that maximises their cross-correlation stays constant over consecutive windows. The published setting (60-s windows, 30-s step, ±30-s lag search, ±1-s tolerance, 4 of 5 windows) was designed for 1-Hz sleep data and is used unchanged on very different signals. We test an adaptive variant, aTDS, that sets the window from each system's autocorrelation (about 30 independent samples per window, Bartlett factor), the lag range from the window, and the tolerance from a coupling-free circular-shift null (the widest tolerance whose chance score stays ≤ 5 %).

On the 35-subject sleep cohort of Bashan et al. (2012), taken from the author's own reproduction of that study, we first replicate the reproduction exactly: all 4 900 per-subject link strengths, the surrogate distributions (median KS 0.02) and the 8.6 % significance threshold. We then compare TDS and aTDS under pre-registered hypotheses. Both recover the known pattern (deep sleep and REM less connected than light sleep and wake). aTDS does not sharpen these sleep-stage contrasts (H5 inconclusive; with the 80 %-purity rule all four gain CIs include 0), but it separates real coupling from its own null better (mean AUC 0.778 vs 0.736; 114 vs 105 significant link-stage cells, most of the gain in deep sleep and wake). Here aTDS's window is set by the 300-s cap, not by the rule (for EEG δ power the rule asks for a median of 83 min), and the 300-s window leaves wake undefined in 25 of 35 subjects under the strict stage rule.

The thesis's sleep-apnea results can only be re-drawn from stored outputs (the input signals are not available), so aTDS could not be applied there. On 30 Garmin runs, aTDS detects speed → heart-rate coupling in 8 runs versus 4 for the app's fixed setting (McNemar p = 0.29), with 1/30 cross-run false positives; this reproduces an earlier analysis on the same data. aTDS helps when the fixed window holds less than one independent sample of the slowest signal and the recording is long enough to afford a longer window; it costs time resolution, delay precision and short states.

1 Introduction

Network physiology treats organ systems as nodes and their interactions as links. Bashan et al. (2012) introduced Time Delay Stability to define those links from continuous recordings: two signals are coupled when the delay τ₀ at which their cross-correlation peaks stays nearly constant from one window to the next. Applied to 1-Hz signals from healthy sleepers (EEG band powers, heart rate, respiration, eye and muscle activity), the resulting network changed with sleep stage, with the fewest links in deep sleep, more in REM, and the most in light sleep and quiet wake (Bashan et al. 2012, Fig. 2). Bartsch et al. (2015) extended the same method, with the same parameters, to brain–organ networks.

The parameters were set for that setting: windows of L = 60 s “which corresponds to the time resolution of the conventional sleep-stage-scoring epochs” with a 30-s overlap (Bashan et al. 2012, Methods), and a delay that stays within ±1 for at least four out of five consecutive segments (Bartsch et al. 2015, Methods). They are written in seconds and samples, not in units of the signals' own time scales. A signal whose memory spans minutes contributes less than one independent sample to a 60-s window; the delay found in each window is then mostly noise, and stability becomes a matter of chance.

The author's MSc thesis (Markuza 2023) applied TDS to polysomnography from sleep-apnea patients. In earlier work on pyTDS, a Python implementation of TDS, we proposed and tested an adaptive version, aTDS (internal reports, see References). This paper asks: on real physiological data with a known answer, does aTDS change what TDS finds, why, and at what cost? It reports a replication, a pre-registered comparison, worked examples on actual signals, and every result that did not favour aTDS.

2 Methods

2.1 Time Delay Stability (TDS)

Both signals are cut into windows of length L with step L/2. Each window is z-scored and the cross-correlation C(τ) is computed for |τ| ≤ τmax; τ₀ is the lag of the largest |C|. A window is labelled stable when, within a run of five consecutive windows, at least four τ₀ values lie within ±tol of one another. The TDS score is the percentage of stable windows. The published setting at 1 Hz is L = 60 s, step 30 s, τmax = 30 s, tol = ±1 s. pyTDS uses circular (periodic-boundary) cross-correlation; the thesis and its reproduction used MATLAB's linear xcorr(…,30,'coeff'). Section 3.1 quantifies the difference.

2.2 Adaptive TDS (aTDS)

aTDS derives one parameter set per system (all signals of one recording), using only per-signal properties and a coupling-free null, so it cannot be tuned toward the coupling being measured (experiments/adaptive_groundtruth/adaptive.py: adaptive_params, rule “v2”).

  • Window. For each signal, the Bartlett factor B = 1 + 2 Σk r(k)², summed over the autocorrelation r(k) up to its first zero crossing, estimates how many samples one independent sample is worth. The raw window is 30·B of the slowest signal (about 30 independent samples), clipped to [20 s, cap]. The cap is a user choice that states the time resolution one accepts; 300 s here, as in the earlier sleep study, and 240 s for Garmin runs.
  • Step and lag. Step = L/2; τmax = (L−1)/2, further capped at half the shortest detected oscillation period.
  • Tolerance. One signal of each pair is circularly shifted by a random large amount, which keeps each signal's own structure but destroys coupling. The tolerance is the largest value whose mean null TDS score stays ≤ 5 %; if even tol = 0 exceeds 5 %, the result is flagged calibration_failed.

Ablations isolate each change: win_only (adaptive window, tol ±1), tol_only (60-s window, calibrated tolerance), v2_cal_lag3 (aTDS with τmax = L/3 and tolerance recalibrated), and caps of 120 s and 600 s.

2.3 Data

  • Bashan-2012 sleep cohort (inputs available). 35 subjects, 10 signals at 1 Hz (HR, respiration, chin and leg EMG, eye movements, EEG δ θ α σ β band power), per-second sleep stage (LS, DS, REM, wake), about 272 h. These are the data of the author's learning reproduction of Bashan et al. (2012), stored in the thesis repository (01 repreduce NCOM). The thesis uses this reproduction only to illustrate the surrogate method (Fig. 11–12, p. 22–23: “taken from the same patient used in [7]”, i.e. Bashan et al.); it is not the thesis research. The MATLAB table objects were decoded with paper/scripts/extract_ncom.py.
  • Thesis sleep-apnea cohort (outputs only). 28 patients from Charité Berlin (2016–19), 14 with AHI > 10 and 14 with AHI ≤ 10, 15 signals at 1 Hz (thesis p. 13). Only stored outputs exist: per-patient full-night 15 × 15 TDS matrices, group-pooled stage maps and ranks. The 1-Hz inputs are not available, so aTDS could not be applied.
  • Garmin runs (inputs available). 41 of the author's runs, 5 channels at 1 Hz (heart rate, speed, cadence, power, climb), GPS-free export. 30 runs to 22 Sep 2026 are usable for the main comparison; 4 later runs form a held-out set.

2.4 Sleep-stage scoring and outcome

A window is pure for a stage when ≥ 80 % of its samples carry that stage. Because a stability label uses five consecutive windows (900 s at a 300-s window, 180 s at 60 s), the primary strict rule counts window i for a stage only if windows i−2…i+2 are all pure for it; the 80 %-only rule is a sensitivity analysis. A stage score needs ≥ 5 counted windows.

Raw TDS percentages are not comparable across methods: a longer window and a wider tolerance change the chance level. The outcome is therefore excess = real score − chance score, where chance is the same pair with one signal circularly shifted (5 shifts), scored on the same windows. Per subject and stage, connectivity is the mean excess over the 35 links used in the reproduction (all pairs except EEG–EEG).

2.5 Hypotheses and statistics

The plan (paper/ANALYSIS_PLAN.md, commits d031557 and earlier) was written before the main runs and checked by an independent reviewer pass on a sample of the real data, which changed the design in six places (surrogate port, pooled threshold mean, the strict stage rule, a per-method null for the network test, same-subject comparisons, a decision rule for H5). Hypotheses, from the direction reported by Bashan et al. (2012): H1 LS > DS, H2 wake > DS, H3 LS > REM, H4 wake > REM. For the published setting these are a replication, not a blind test. H5 (the aTDS claim): the paired effect size dz is larger with aTDS; supported if the gain CI is above 0 in ≥ 3 of 4 contrasts, refuted if below 0 in ≥ 2, otherwise inconclusive. H6: aTDS separates real from null at least as well (mean AUC). Paired dz = mean/SD of within-subject differences; 95 % CIs from 10 000 subject bootstraps; Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction over H1–H4. Gains are computed on subjects defined under both methods. Network level: per link and stage, AUC = P(real subject score > null score) against 50 circular-shift draws per subject; one-sided Mann–Whitney p < 10⁻³ marks a significant link-stage cell.

3 Results

3.1 Replication of the Bashan-2012 reproduction

A line-by-line Python port of the reproduction's MATLAB TDS (paper/scripts/thesis_port.py) reproduces all 4 900 stored link strengths (35 subjects × 35 links × 4 stages) exactly (maximum absolute difference 0.0). The surrogate procedure (GetSurPatient.m with its second half-pass and randomizeSurData.m) was ported in full; 2 000 new surrogate subjects match the stored 2 000 in distribution (per link-stage KS statistic median 0.020, max 0.049; mean link strength 2.441 vs 2.442; Fig. 1A).

The significance threshold (smallest TDS % above which every link is significant, t-test p < 10⁻³) is 8.6 % in the stored output and 8.6 % again from our surrogates when the pooled link means are built from the reproduction's own Stages_cell.mat. The thesis text reports 9 % (Fig. 12), consistent with 8.6 % rounded. Pooling the means from All_data_clean.mat instead gives 7.1 %, because that file's stage alignment differs from the raw-file pipeline used for the means (Fig. 1B).

pyTDS at the same setting does not equal the MATLAB code: across the 4 900 values r = 0.950 and pyTDS is 3.3 points lower on average (stability anchor “any”; r = 0.905, −7.4 points with the causal anchor “end”). The difference is circular versus linear cross-correlation. On the classic δ–σ benchmark in pyTDS/data, the reproduction's MATLAB port gives 61.8 %, pyTDS 58.1 % (default) and 43.5 % (anchor “end”); the 43.6 % figure in the pyTDS documentation is therefore matched only with anchor “end”.

3.2 Worked example: how each method reads one night

The example was chosen by a rule fixed before it was computed (plan, section R5): the subject whose aTDS-minus-TDS gain in LS−DS connectivity is closest to the cohort median (subject ), and within it the link closest to that subject's median link gain (). The displayed 40 minutes start 600 s before the first light → deep sleep transition. aTDS chose a 300-s window (the cap; the Bartlett rule asked for ), a ±149-s lag range and a ±-s tolerance (Fig. 2E).

3.3 TDS vs aTDS across sleep stages (pre-registered)

Parameters. In all 35 subjects aTDS took the 300-s cap, a ±149-s lag range and a tolerance of ±14 to ±17 s (median 15); calibration never failed. So on these data “aTDS” means a 300-s window with a calibrated tolerance; the Bartlett rule itself would ask for far longer windows (Section 3.4).

H1–H4 hold for both methods (Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon p < 0.05 for every contrast, both stage rules; Fig. 4). With the published setting, under the strict rule: LS > DS dz 1.95 [1.59, 2.56], wake > DS 1.16 [0.85, 1.63], LS > REM 1.40 [1.09, 1.90], wake > REM 0.74 [0.38, 1.25]. Deep sleep is the least connected stage under both methods (Fig. 3).

H5 is inconclusive. On the same subjects, aTDS's dz is lower for LS > DS (gain −0.20 [−0.91, 0.45], n = 30) and LS > REM (−0.20 [−0.66, 0.36]) and higher for the two wake contrasts (+0.80 [0.08, 3.17], n = 6; +0.62 [0.09, 1.67], n = 9). The two positive gains rest on 6 and 9 subjects, because a 300-s window leaves too few pure wake windows in most nights. Under the 80 %-only rule (wake defined in 20–21 subjects), all four gains are negative and every CI includes 0 (−0.19 to −0.36). The pre-registered rule therefore gives “inconclusive”, and the honest reading is that aTDS does not sharpen the stage contrasts in this cohort.

Exploratory, not pre-registered as a claim: keeping the 60-s window and only calibrating the tolerance (tol_only, which chose ±2 s in every subject) raises the wake contrasts on 29–34 subjects: wake > DS gain +0.52 [0.25, 0.93] and wake > REM +0.44 [0.17, 0.78] under the strict rule, +0.60 and +0.52 under the 80 % rule. The long window with the published ±1-s tolerance (win_only) is the worst variant, as in the earlier sleep study.

H6 holds. Against each method's own circular-shift null, aTDS separates real coupling better: mean AUC over 140 link-stage cells 0.778 vs 0.736 (Fig. 5). The gain is largest in wake (0.808 vs 0.713) and deep sleep (0.655 vs 0.623; significant cells 17 vs 12 of 35). Significant cells: 114 vs 105 of 140. The lag-capped variant is similar (0.785); win_only is worse than published (0.673).

3.4 Why: independent samples per window

The Bartlett factor turns each signal's memory into a count of independent samples. Median over the 35 subjects, a 60-s window holds 0.36 independent samples of EEG δ power, 0.82 of θ, 0.93 of heart rate, 2.0 of α, 2.4 of respiration, 4.4 of σ, 5.3 of β, 8.2 of eye movements, 10.9 of chin EMG and 16.4 of leg EMG (Fig. 6). For the slow signals the delay found in a 60-s window rests on less than one independent sample, so it is close to noise; for the muscle signals it does not. aTDS sizes the window for the slowest signal of the system, which here would mean windows far beyond 300 s (subject medians of the Bartlett window: δ 4 994 s ≈ 83 min, θ 2 192 s, HR 1 939 s). The 300-s cap therefore set every aTDS window, and the cap, not the rule, decided the trade-off between time resolution and reliability.

That trade-off is visible in Fig. 5: at 300 s most wake bouts are too short to yield five pure windows in a row, and a 600-s cap leaves deep sleep defined in only 2 subjects (strict rule). A 120-s cap keeps more subjects and gives dz similar to the published setting (LS > DS 2.19, wake > DS 1.39); its network-level AUC was not computed.

3.6 The thesis sleep-apnea results, re-drawn from stored outputs

These are the thesis's own results. Without input signals they cannot be recomputed with TDS or aTDS; what can be done is to recompute the statistics from the stored outputs and check them. The stored rank arrays match the stored group stage maps exactly (two copies of ranks_sick&healthy.mat exist; the one in TDS imp/, where the analysis scripts run, matches, the other is an older version). Apnea patients' pooled link strengths are higher in wake, REM and deep sleep (thesis signed-rank p = 3.2·10⁻¹⁸, 6.5·10⁻¹⁰, 6.7·10⁻¹⁶) but not in light sleep (p = 0.15); KS tests on the same values are not significant in any stage (p 0.06–0.73).

The thesis test sorts each group's 105 link strengths before pairing them, so it compares the k-th strongest link of one group with the k-th strongest of the other, not the same link. Pairing the same link instead (a check added here) gives the same picture: wake p = 3.3·10⁻¹¹ (81 % of links stronger in apnea), deep sleep 7.8·10⁻⁶ (70 %), REM 8.1·10⁻⁵ (66 %), light sleep 0.38 (60 %). Across the 28 patients, the number of full-night links at the 7.8 % threshold rises with AHI (Spearman ρ = 0.52, p = 0.005; median 58 vs 47.5 links). The thesis's own Fig. 27 uses stage-specific networks, which are not stored per patient; this full-night version is a stand-in, not a reproduction.

3.7 Garmin runs

Expectations were written before the run (plan, E1–E5) from the earlier analysis of the same data, so this section is a reproduction, not a new test. The comparison script was rerun unchanged (GarminTDS/experiments/adaptive_compare/compare.py; the empty run-list file of the latest export was rebuilt from the data keys) and gave the earlier numbers exactly. A detection is speed → HR coupling above 100 circular-shift chance scores (p < 0.05), speed leading, lag not at the edge of the search.

  • E1 holds: aTDS 8/30 vs the app's fixed 120 / 60 / ±40 / ±2 setting 4/30; 6 runs only by aTDS, 2 only by the app setting (exact McNemar p = 0.29).
  • E2 holds: cross-run false positives (speed of one run vs HR of another) 1/30 for aTDS, 0/30 for the app setting.
  • E3 holds: aTDS took the 240-s cap on all 30 runs and a tolerance of ±2 to ±28 s (median ±14); the spread of the lag across runs widened (IQR 1.9 → 7 s).
  • E4 holds: held-out runs 2/4 vs 0/4 (lags 15 and 21 s).
  • E5 holds: the same 240-s window and ±14-s tolerance with the lag limited to 80 s (window/3) detects 4/30, so the wide lag range carries the gain: with a loose tolerance, a wider search makes chance stability rarer.

The worked example (Fig. 9) is the median-length run among the 8 where the methods disagree, chosen by the pre-set rule. It also shows the main caveat: a 240-s window on a 27-minute run gives only about a dozen windows, so all 100 chance scores are 0 and a single stable stretch makes p ≈ 0.01.

4 Discussion

What aTDS changes, and when it helps

aTDS replaces three fixed numbers with quantities measured on the signals: a window long enough to contain about 30 independent samples, a lag range that grows with it, and a tolerance set so that uncoupled signals score ≤ 5 % by chance. The evidence here and in the earlier work points to one mechanism: when the fixed window holds fewer than one or two independent samples of the slowest signal, τ₀ per window is mostly noise and stability is luck. A longer window fixes that, but only if the tolerance is widened to match; a long window with ±1 s is the worst variant on both sleep datasets. With a loose tolerance, a wide lag search also matters, because it makes chance stability rarer (Garmin, E5).

Gains were large where the fixed window was much shorter than the system's memory and nothing else changed: synthetic slow coupling, the Sleep-EDF EEG network, the Garmin runs. On the Bashan-2012 cohort the picture is mixed. The network-level separation from chance improved, mostly in deep sleep and wake. The stage contrasts did not sharpen. Two features of this cohort explain much of that: it mixes fast signals (EMG, eye movements, which already have 8–16 independent samples per minute) with slow ones, so one system-wide window is too long for some pairs; and its sleep stages, especially wake, come in bouts that a 300-s window with a five-window stability rule cannot resolve.

When it hurts

  • Time resolution. The cap sets it. At 300 s, wake was undefined in 25/35 subjects (strict rule); at 600 s deep sleep was defined in 2. Short states and short recordings suffer.
  • Delay precision. A ±14–15-s tolerance accepts delays that wander by that much: on the Garmin runs the lag IQR across runs widened from 1.9 to 7 s.
  • Few windows. On short records the chance distribution collapses to 0 and p-values rest on one stable stretch (Fig. 9).
  • Oscillations. Delays near half an oscillation period cannot be pinned down (Rulkov delay 16).

Limitations

  • The thesis's own question (apnea vs control) could not be tested with aTDS: the input signals are unavailable.
  • One cohort of 35 subjects with mixed signal types; per-pair windows were not tried.
  • The Garmin result is a reproduction on the same runs that suggested aTDS's Garmin settings; only 4 held-out runs exist.
  • pyTDS's circular cross-correlation differs from the MATLAB code (r = 0.95); both methods in Section 3.3 use pyTDS, so the comparison is internally consistent but not identical to the MATLAB numbers.
  • The strict stage rule removes more windows from aTDS than from TDS; the 80 %-only rule is reported alongside and leads to the same conclusion.

5 Conclusion

The author's reproduction of Bashan et al. (2012) replicates exactly, including its 8.6 % significance threshold. Measured against the known sleep-stage pattern, adaptive TDS is not uniformly better: it separates real coupling from chance more clearly, but it does not sharpen stage contrasts in this cohort, and its 300-s window costs short stages. Its benefit is largest when the fixed 60-s window holds less than one independent sample of the slowest signal and the recording is long, as for slow EEG networks and running data. The window cap should stay an explicit user choice that states the time resolution one needs. A tolerance calibrated on a coupling-free null is the part worth keeping on every dataset; here it alone improved the wake contrasts at the published window.

Appendix B · Discrepancies found and how they were resolved

  • Threshold 8.6 % vs 9 % vs 7.1 %. 8.6 % is stored and reproduced; 9 % in the thesis text is consistent with rounding; 7.1 % arises only when the pooled means come from All_data_clean.mat.
  • Stored p-values. The stored per-link p-values are not exactly reproducible from the stored real and surrogate arrays in the same file (3 of 140 significance calls differ); they were probably computed on a different surrogate draw. The threshold is unaffected.
  • Two rank files. Only the copy in TDS imp/ matches the stored stage maps; it is the one used.
  • 43.6 % benchmark. Reached by pyTDS only with stability_anchor="end" (43.5 %); the default gives 58.1 %.
  • Thesis cohort size. The thesis text (p. 13) and the stored outputs (28 per-patient matrices, AHI in the file names) agree on 28 patients, 14 + 14.
