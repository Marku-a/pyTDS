THOUGHT:
The paper proposes adaptive TDS (aTDS): three data-driven rules for the window length (30 x max Bartlett factor, clipped to [61, 0.1T]), the lag range ((L-1)/2, capped at half a detected rhythm period), and the stability tolerance (the largest tolerance for which circularly rotated copies keep the mean chance score + 2 SE at or below 5 %). The 4-of-5 stability rule is kept. The evaluation is long and unusually candid: the rules are tested one at a time, combined in a slow-down test, run on five time-varying delay patterns and several signal classes, and applied to a 1-Hz sleep EEG benchmark, a 35-subject PSG cohort, 53 BIDMC ECG/PPG recordings at 125 Hz, and hydrological, energy and fuel-price records.

My main intuition is that this is careful, honest engineering with limited conceptual novelty. The paper's own strongest baseline undercuts much of the headline gain. "Rescaled fixed TDS" multiplies the original parameters by a factor taken from the 1/e autocorrelation time. It matches aTDS in AUC in the slow-down test (it is slightly higher at r = 2 and 4) and on the ECG data (0.962 vs 0.963). The authors admit that "matching the time scale is the decisive factor". Most of the AUC gap to fixed TDS (0.92 vs 0.59, 50 vs 14 patients) therefore comes from comparing against a fixed setting applied far outside its design regime. Any practitioner who knew the sampling rate would rescale. What aTDS adds beyond rescaling is narrower: (i) the rhythm cap, (ii) a calibrated tolerance that controls the chance level of controls (ECG controls' 95th percentile 1.3 % vs 39 %), and (iii) better delay recovery at r = 8. These are real but modest benefits. The ECG control-level advantage also partly reflects the rhythm cap, which the rescaled baseline lacks by construction. A rescaled baseline with a rhythm cap, or with a calibrated tolerance, would show which component matters.

Technical points to note:
- The window rule uses a truncated Bartlett sum at the first zero crossing. For long-memory and seasonal signals this is admitted to be record-length dependent and to fail (seasonal catchment). The constant 30 is a convention, and Table 8 shows strong sensitivity (AUC at r = 4 moves from 0.72 to 0.99 between 15 and 60 independent samples). So the window rule is effectively a trade-off knob rather than a principled choice.
- The tolerance budget is a mean window-level false-stability rate, not a link-level error rate. In the delay-pattern tests aTDS's false stability is 4-6 % against under 1 % for fixed TDS. A wide tolerance (+-13 for a delay of 20) changes what "stable" means. The authors handle this partly by assessing delay accuracy separately, but scores are not comparable to the literature.
- Calibration fails at t = 0 in 12 of 53 ECG pairs. Per-pair parameters put true pairs and controls on different scales, although the one-set analysis addresses this.
- Using the non-causal anchor by default while reproducing the MATLAB benchmark only with the causal anchor is acceptable because it is applied consistently, but it adds confusion.
- In the ECG data, agreement with the beat-by-beat pulse arrival time is worse for aTDS (24/53 vs 32/41). The 0.04 s full-record alignment reference is favourable to long windows.
- Within the design regime (sleep cohort), there is no gain, and wake contrasts are weaker.
- No comparison with standard alternatives for the real goal, such as full-record or windowed GCC/PHAT delay estimation, or a simple surrogate-thresholded windowed cross-correlation. The novelty is confined to TDS parameterisation, and the audience is the network-physiology community.
- Rotation surrogates are standard. The effective-sample-size rationale is standard. The combination is sensible but incremental.
- The sleep cohort comes from an MSc project and is not redistributed. Reproducibility is otherwise good (code, seeds, raw JSON).

Presentation is dense and rigorous but very hard to read. Many numbers are packed into prose, much detail sits in appendices, and the abstract is overloaded. Table 7 is a useful honest summary.

Overall: sound and transparent, with valuable negative results (silent lag-range failure, sleep cohort no gain). The incremental contribution over a simple data-driven rescaling is small and only partly isolated. I lean towards a weak reject (5): the main claimed gain is largely against a strawman, and the components that distinguish aTDS from rescaling are not ablated against that baseline.
