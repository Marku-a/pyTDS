THOUGHT:
The paper addresses a real and under-discussed practical problem: TDS parameters (60 / ±30 / ±1 samples) are tied to 1-Hz sleep data, and applying them unchanged at other time scales or sampling rates degrades detection and, more worryingly, can return high scores with wrong (even sign-flipped) delays. The proposed fix, aTDS, consists of three heuristics: (i) window = 30 x max Bartlett factor (effective-sample-size argument), (ii) lag range = (L-1)/2 with a half-period cap for rhythmic signals, (iii) tolerance = largest value keeping the chance score of circularly rotated copies below a 5 % window-level budget (mean + 2 SE).

My main intuitions:
- The silent-failure demonstration (Section 2.10) is valuable but, as the authors concede, true by construction; its main value is showing scores remain 72-96 % while the delay is wrong. That is a useful warning to practitioners.
- The core novelty is modest. Rule (i) is a direct use of Bartlett/Bayley-Hammersley effective sample size; rule (ii) keeps the same L/2 ratio as fixed TDS; rule (iii) is a standard surrogate calibration. Crucially, the authors' own "rescaled fixed TDS" baseline (one data-estimated factor) captures most of the detection gain and is slightly better at r=2 and r=4. So the headline gains vs fixed TDS (AUC 0.92 vs 0.59; 50 vs 14 patients) mostly measure "matching the time scale" rather than the specific aTDS design. The differential contribution of the rhythm cap and calibrated tolerance rests largely on one dataset (ECG: control 95th percentile 39 % vs 1.3 %, 43 vs 50 patients), and there rescaled fixed TDS actually finds the delay more often (41/53 vs 38/50), which Table 7 does not reflect ("equal; controls better").
- The oracle outperforms aTDS at every r >= 2 by using much longer windows; the "30 independent samples" constant is admitted to be a convention and Table 8 shows 45 or 60 detect clearly better at slow scales. So aTDS is a reasonable default, not an optimised procedure. The justification of 30 (noise max ~0.47) is hand-wavy.
- Within the design regime (sleep networks), aTDS gives no gain and loses power for the wake contrast; the uncapped window (48 min) cannot resolve sleep stages at all. Thus for the main application area of TDS (network physiology of sleep), the method needs an analyst-chosen cap, which undermines the "data-driven" claim. The stage-aware variant helps but is introduced ad hoc.
- The tolerance budget is window-level, not link-level; false stability rises to 4-4.3 % (vs <1 % for fixed TDS), and boundary inheritance gives 6.1 % in on/off. Calibration failed in 12/53 ECG pairs; these were silently scored with tol 0, mixing parameter regimes. Per-pair parameters also place true pairs and controls on different scales (acknowledged, then repeated with one set).
- Real-world "other fields" evaluation: 3 of 5 records recovered; the seasonal catchment and weekly fuel prices fail. Ground truth is weak ("about 8 h", "0-3 days").
- Delay accuracy on ECG relative to beat-by-beat pulse arrival time is worse than fixed TDS (24/53 vs 32/41), and the use of |C| can lock half a beat away; the choice of the "full-record alignment" reference partly defines success in aTDS' favour.
- Strengths in rigor: careful paired bootstrap, equivalence tests, Wilson intervals, separation of training/test data, honest reporting of negative results, sensitivity analyses, reproducible code. Presentation is dense but clear; however, the paper is very long relative to its conceptual content, with many numbers repeated across text, Tables 2, 3, 5, 7.
- Minor: the causal vs non-causal anchor discrepancy (43.5 vs 58.1 % on benchmark) is important for comparability with literature and deserves more prominence; Bartlett truncation at first zero crossing is a heuristic that fails for seasonal/long-memory data (acknowledged); single-configuration test of indirect links.

Overall: a careful, honest engineering contribution that will be useful to TDS users, but the methodological novelty is incremental, the advantage over a simple data-driven rescaling is limited and mixed, and in the canonical application domain it provides no gain. I lean to reject for a general venue, borderline (5).
