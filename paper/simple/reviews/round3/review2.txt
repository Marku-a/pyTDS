THOUGHT:
The paper proposes adaptive TDS (aTDS), three data-driven rules for the window length, lag range and tolerance of Time Delay Stability. The window holds 30 Bartlett-effective samples of the slowest signal. The lag range is half the window, capped at half a detected rhythm period. The tolerance is the largest one that keeps the mean chance score of circularly rotated copies, plus 2 SE, at or below 5 %. The 4-of-5 stability rule is kept unchanged.

My first impression is that this is an unusually careful and honest validation paper with a modest methodological core. Each rule is tested in isolation, against a manually rescaled setting, a data-rescaled setting and an oracle. Negative and null results are reported prominently: there is no gain on 1-Hz sleep data, a weaker wake contrast, the oracle is clearly better, the seasonal catchment fails, there is no delay for fuel prices, and the rhythm cap excludes delays beyond half a period. Table 9 is an exemplary summary. Claims are generally hedged appropriately.

The central question for significance is what aTDS adds beyond the obvious fix of rescaling the fixed parameters by the time scale. The authors confront this themselves. Rescaled fixed TDS, a one-factor rescaling estimated from the 1/e correlation time, matches aTDS in AUC in the slow-down test (slightly better at r = 2 and 4, worse at r = 8 and 16) and on the ECG cohort (0.962 vs 0.963). The remaining claimed advantages are:
- delay recovery at r = 8 (85 % vs 60 %);
- the ECG control level (95th percentile 39 % vs 1.3 %) and therefore 50 vs 43 patients above the control threshold.

The ECG advantage rests on a baseline that has an uncalibrated tolerance (±8 samples) and no rhythm cap. A natural ablation is missing: rescaled window plus rhythm cap plus calibrated tolerance, or equivalently aTDS with the window replaced by the rescaled one. Without it, the contribution of the Bartlett-based window over a simpler 1/e-time-based window is not demonstrated. The added value may lie entirely in the tolerance-calibration and rhythm-cap components, which are fairly standard ideas (surrogate calibration, period cap).

The 43 vs 50 patient comparison is also not tested statistically, and it is relative to a 95th percentile estimated from only 53 controls.

The ECG task itself is fairly easy. ECG and PPG of the same patient share the heart rate, while controls pair patients with different heart rates. Any full-record coherence or cross-correlation would likely separate them. No simple full-record baseline is reported, although the authors concede that full-record estimators are preferable for constant delays.

In the per-pair setting, calibration failed in 12 of 53 true pairs because rotations of a periodic signal stay aligned by chance. This exposes a structural weakness of the rotation null for rhythmic signals.

Other technical points:
- Truncating the Bartlett sum at the first zero crossing is a heuristic, and it fails for seasonal and long-memory signals, as the authors acknowledge.
- The design constants (30, 61, 10 % of T, 0.3 rhythm threshold, 5 %, z = 2, 50 pairs, 20 rotations) are conventions. The sensitivity analysis shows AUC depends strongly on the constant 30 at slow scales (0.717 at 15, 0.991 at 60 for r = 4), so "30" is a substantive tuning choice rather than a neutral default.
- The calibrated tolerance grows with time scale (±136 s in the uncapped sleep analysis, ±56 at r = 16). TDS scores then mean something different and cannot be compared with the literature. This undermines one of the stated motivations, reproducibility and comparability.
- The 5 % budget controls the mean window-level false stability, not link-level error, and this is correctly noted.
- The uncapped window is useless for stage-resolved sleep analysis (48 min). The method therefore needs an analyst-chosen cap in the main application domain, so it is not fully parameter-free.
- The benchmark reproduction depends on the causal versus non-causal anchor (43.5 vs 58.1 %). Disclosing this is good, but it means the default pyTDS does not reproduce the original MATLAB implementation.
- No comparison with established time-delay estimators (generalised cross-correlation, Knapp–Carter) or other coupling measures, even as context for delay accuracy.

Presentation is clear but extremely dense with numbers. Some counts are hard to reconcile across text, tables and figures:
- ECG delay agreement appears as 40/53 per pair in Table 6 and Table 9 and as 38/50 for one set (Table 3, Table 9).
- Fig. 10 says aTDS reported delays for 53 patients, but which setting is meant is unclear.

The abstract is long and reads like a results list. The sleep cohort comes from the author's MSc project and is not redistributed.

Overall, the work is thorough and honest, and it is a useful practical contribution for TDS users and software. The methodological novelty is limited, though. The paper's own evidence shows that a simple one-factor rescaling captures most of the benefit, and the specific value of the Bartlett-based window is not isolated. On 1-Hz physiological data, the domain of TDS, there is no gain. I lean toward a borderline reject (5) for a selective venue. It would be a reasonable fit for a methods or software journal after the missing ablation and a simple full-record baseline are added.
