THOUGHT:
The paper proposes adaptive Time Delay Stability (aTDS): three data-driven rules replacing the sample-based TDS constants (window 60, lag +/-30, tolerance +/-1). The window holds 30 "independent samples" of the slowest signal (Bartlett factor truncated at the first zero crossing). The lag range is half the window, capped at half a detected rhythm period. The tolerance is the largest value for which the mean chance score of circularly rotated copies, plus 2 SE, stays within a 5 % false-stability budget. The 4-of-5 rule is unchanged.

My intuitions:
- The problem is real and practically relevant. TDS parameters are in samples, people apply them unchanged, and the "silent failure" demonstration (high TDS scores with a boundary or wrong-sign delay when the lag range is too short) is a useful, citable warning for the network-physiology community.
- The conceptual novelty is modest. Each rule is a reasonable engineering heuristic (effective sample size, Nyquist-like lag bound, surrogate calibration), not a new methodological idea. The paper's own strongest baseline, a one-factor "rescaled fixed TDS" driven by the 1/e correlation time, matches aTDS in AUC in the slow-down test and on the ECG data. It is even slightly better at r = 2 and 4. So the main detection gain comes from "match the time scale", which is almost self-evident once stated. What aTDS adds over that is the calibrated tolerance (much lower control scores on ECG: 95th percentile 1.3 % vs 39 %) and the rhythm cap. Both are shown in essentially one dataset.
- The evaluation is unusually thorough and honest: one-rule-at-a-time ablations, an oracle upper bound, sensitivity to every design constant, equivalence testing (TOST), a summary table that also lists the cases where aTDS is worse, and frank negatives (no gain on 1-Hz sleep data, weaker wake contrast, fuel prices unresolved, a seasonal catchment needing a cap). This is a strength, but it also shows the method's limited reach: in the design regime it brings nothing, and on the sleep cohort the uncapped window (48 min) is useless for stage resolution.
- Technical concerns:
  (i) The rotation null with an exclusion zone of max(L, T/10) is a weak null for nonstationary, seasonal or long-memory signals. The 2 SE bound is computed over rotations that are not independent across shifts of the same pair, so "budget holds on new recordings" is supported empirically (3.3-4.4 %) only for stationary AR-type signals.
  (ii) The 5 % budget controls a mean window-level stability, not link-level error. The paper says so, but per-link inference then relies on a separate rotation test whose behaviour is shown only lightly (one common-driver configuration).
  (iii) A tolerance of +/-13 for a delay of 20, up to tau_max/4, changes what "stable" means. aTDS scores are not comparable with the literature; the authors acknowledge this, but it weakens the "TDS extension" framing.
  (iv) With the circular correlation and lags up to (L-1)/2, the wrapped portion at long lags is large, which explains the falling scores at long delays. A linear (zero-padded) correlation or a lag range tied to overlap would be worth discussing.
  (v) In the ECG analysis the reference delay is itself a full-record cross-correlation peak, so agreement with it partly favours longer windows by construction. Against the physiologically meaningful pulse arrival time, aTDS is worse (24/53 vs 32/41). Per-pair calibration failed in 12/53 pairs (scored with tol 0).
  (vi) The "fixed TDS at 125 Hz" baseline is close to a straw man. The rescaled baseline is the meaningful one and is used in only two experiments. No comparison is made with other windowed delay estimators (e.g., GCC-PHAT or the delay-correlation landscape).
- Reproducibility: the code and seeds are public, but the 35-subject sleep cohort is private MSc data. The non-causal default anchor gives 58.1 % vs 43.6 % for the original, which may confuse users.
- Presentation: precise but very dense. The text has many numbers and long sentences. Some section headings are orphaned ("Sleep networks in a 35-subject cohort." separated from its text), the Figure 10 caption lacks its (a) label, and Table 9 mixes heterogeneous criteria for "better". AI-assisted drafting is disclosed.

Overall: a careful, well-supported, practically useful methods paper with moderate novelty. The main claims are supported and appropriately hedged. Significance is mostly for TDS users. I lean to a 6, Accept, with revisions on null robustness, baselines and concision.
