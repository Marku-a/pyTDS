THOUGHT:
The paper proposes adaptive TDS (aTDS): three data-driven rules for the window length (30 Bartlett-effective independent samples of the slowest signal), the lag range (half the window, capped at half a detected rhythm period) and the stability tolerance (largest tolerance whose rotation-null chance score, upper bound C + 2 SE, stays within a 5 % window-level budget). It is evaluated per rule on AR(1) simulations, on a slow-down test against fixed, rescaled-fixed, manually rescaled and oracle TDS, on time-varying delays, on a sleep-EEG benchmark pair, on a 35-subject sleep cohort, on 53 BIDMC ECG/PPG records at 125 Hz, and on five records from hydrology, energy and fuel prices.

My intuitions:
- The problem is real but narrow. TDS parameters counted in samples clearly do not transfer across sampling rates. The paper's own most informative baseline, rescaled fixed TDS (one factor from the 1/e autocorrelation time), matches aTDS in AUC in the slow-down test (it is slightly higher at r = 2 and 4) and on the ECG data (0.962 vs 0.963). The main headline gains (AUC 0.92 vs 0.59; 50/53 vs 14/53 patients) are therefore mostly against a strawman: fixed TDS applied unchanged far outside its design regime. The author says this openly, but it caps the significance. What aTDS adds beyond rescaling is narrower: the rhythm cap (unambiguous delays in periodic signals) and the calibrated tolerance (low control scores). The ECG ablation does support the tolerance rule well (control 95th percentile 39 % down to about 1.6 %).
- Originality is modest. Bartlett effective sample size, circular-shift surrogates and half-window lag limits are all standard, and the paper says "none of these ingredients is new". The contribution is engineering a sensible default procedure.
- Technical concerns:
  (a) The window rule's justification is heuristic. The constant 30 is a convention. The Cauchy–Schwarz argument holds for the full sums but not for the first-zero-truncated estimator actually used. Bartlett's variance formula covers independent series, not the coupled pairs that matter. It also fails for seasonal and long-memory signals (window of about 3 years for L0123002; 48-minute windows in sleep).
  (b) The tolerance scales with the time scale (±56 at r = 16). Scores are then not comparable across systems, and "stability" becomes loose. At L = 1083 a ±56 tolerance with a 541 lag range is a much weaker notion of a stable delay than ±1 at L = 60. The 5 % budget is a mean, window-level quantity, not a link-level error rate. Link-level false-positive control is left to a separate test that is only applied in some experiments.
  (c) The tolerance is calibrated on rotations of the true (coupled) pairs. The rotation keeps each autocorrelation but not, for example, nonstationarity aligned across signals (shared trends, shared state changes), so the null may be anti-conservative in recorded data. Also, it is unclear whether rotating within pooled multi-subject data interacts with record boundaries.
  (d) The non-causal anchor (the default) uses look-ahead and inflates scores (58.1 % vs 43.5 % on the benchmark). Long windows plus look-ahead inherit stability near regime boundaries (6.1 % false stability in off blocks).
  (e) Delay correctness, a central motivation ("a high TDS score can coexist with an incorrect delay"), is weaker on real data than the abstract suggests. In the ECG data aTDS agrees with the beat-by-beat pulse arrival time less often than fixed TDS. The reference used instead (full-record cross-correlation within half a beat) is partly circular with the cap. On fuel prices no delay is found, and one catchment fails without a manual cap.
  (f) The sleep cohort, the native domain of TDS, shows no benefit and some loss (wake power). Seasonal, rhythmic and stage-resolved settings need manual caps. So the "data-driven, parameter-free" promise holds only in part.
  (g) The simulations rely mostly on linear AR(1) Gaussian coupling, where a full-record cross-correlation delay estimator would already do well. A comparison with simple alternatives (full-record xcorr with a block-bootstrap or rotation test, or windowed xcorr with effective-df tests) is missing. Such a comparison would show whether the TDS framing adds value at all in the new regimes.
- Strengths: an unusually honest, carefully hedged paper. It uses per-rule isolated evaluation, equivalence testing (TOST), bootstrap and Wilson intervals, held-out splits for ECG, an oracle upper bound, open code with fixed seeds, and explicit limitations. The silent-failure demonstration of the fixed lag range (high scores, wrong and even opposite-signed delays) is a useful practical warning.
- Presentation is dense but clear. There are many numbers and heavy reliance on the supplement (S1–S9). Some definitions, for example "delay found", differ between sections.

Overall: the work is solid and reproducible, but the incremental advance over a trivial data-driven rescaling is small. The only real-data gain beyond rescaling is a lower control level. In the design regime there is no gain, and several real-data cases need manual caps. I lean towards a borderline reject (5); a venue focused on tools or software could reasonably accept it.
