THOUGHT:
The paper proposes adaptive TDS (aTDS): three data-driven rules for the window length (30 "independent samples" of the slowest signal via a truncated Bartlett factor), the lag range (half the window, capped at half a rhythm period) and the stability tolerance (largest tolerance keeping C + 2 SE of rotated copies under a 5 % window-level false-stability budget). It is evaluated on AR(1) simulations (slow-down test, lag-range test, time-varying delays), a benchmark EEG pair, a 35-subject sleep cohort, a 53-patient ECG/PPG data set at 125 Hz and five records from hydrology, energy and fuel prices.

My first impression is that this is a careful, unusually honest methods paper with a modest contribution. The authors say plainly that none of the ingredients is new. They also say that a one-factor, data-driven rescaling of fixed TDS captures most of the detection gain, that aTDS brings no gain in the 1-Hz sleep design regime, and that an oracle does better. That candour is a strength. It also bounds the significance: the headline result (AUC 0.92 vs 0.59 at r = 4; 50 vs 14 patients) mostly measures fixed TDS applied outside the conditions it was designed for, not the specific rules of aTDS. The fair baseline is rescaled fixed TDS. Against it, aTDS is equal or slightly worse in AUC at r = 2 and 4, better only at r = 8, and slightly worse again at r = 16. On ECG the AUCs are identical.

What the specific rules add over rescaling:
- Lag-range rule. The lag range follows the window by the ratio fixed TDS already uses. Its only new part is the rhythm cap. The demonstration that a ±30 search cannot find delays beyond 30 is true by construction. The useful point, that the failure is silent and the score stays high, is a fair warning but not a methodological result.
- Tolerance rule. Its benefit in simulation is not significant in AUC (+0.04 at r = 4 and 8). It is marginally negative at r = 1 and lowers AUC at r = 16 (0.65 vs 0.72 for the mean-based variant). Its clearest benefit is the low control level on ECG. That is real, but it is shown on one data set where the signals are periodic.
- Window rule. The constant 30 is admitted to be a convention. Taking the maximum over signals makes the slowest signal dictate the window for the whole network, which produced a 48-minute window in the sleep cohort and a three-year window in one catchment. The Cauchy–Schwarz argument holds only under the null and for untruncated sums, as the authors acknowledge.

Technical concerns I want to raise:
1. The tolerance grows with the time scale (±56 at r = 16, ±27 at r = 8). The 4-of-5 rule then becomes a much looser "stability" criterion. In the time-varying tests, aTDS false stability in controls is about 4 % against about 0.9 % for fixed TDS. aTDS scores are therefore not comparable across data sets or with the literature (acknowledged). This weakens the stated goal of a reproducible TDS procedure: two users with different data get different stability definitions.
2. ECG delay. The median pulse arrival time (0.53 s) lies outside the aTDS lag range (±0.34 s), because the rhythm cap sets the range to half a beat (0.64 s / 2). The reported delay is therefore an alias modulo one beat, about −0.11 s. This is the same kind of silent delay error that the paper uses to motivate itself. The authors switch the reference to "full-record alignment within half a beat", which makes the comparison circular with respect to the cap. They concede that against the physiological pulse arrival time, fixed TDS agrees more often. This should be discussed openly in the main text, not left to the supplement.
3. Rotation null. A circular rotation of a coupled pair is used for calibration (e.g. pooled over the true ECG pairs). For non-stationary or periodic signals a rotation can re-align rhythms, which the authors note as calibration failure. For non-stationary records it can also join incompatible segments. A comparison with IAAFT or bivariate surrogates, or a justification that the result does not depend on the choice of null, would help.
4. The simulations are dominated by linear Gaussian AR(1) with τc and d tied (d = 5r). Rulkov maps and others are only in the supplement. The comparison of time-varying delays is against fixed TDS mismatched to τc = 8. The fair comparison would be rescaled fixed TDS.
5. The rescaled baseline uses the 1/e crossing and a divisor of 2 that is hand-tuned to r = 1 of the authors' own simulation. On real data its controls are poor partly because its tolerance is not calibrated. A "rescaled + calibrated tolerance" baseline is shown only on ECG, where it nearly matches aTDS. This suggests the paper could be simplified to "rescale, then calibrate the tolerance".
6. Results from other fields: 3 of 5 recovered, one requires a manual cap, one cannot be resolved at weekly sampling. This is anecdotal evidence.
7. Much essential evidence (sensitivity to constants, per-pair results, stage contrasts, oracle details) is in supplementary sections I cannot inspect.

Clarity is good overall, but the text is dense, with many numbers in the prose. Experiment design is sound: fixed seeds, held-out parameter selection, bootstrap and Wilson intervals, and TOST equivalence testing. Significance is narrow (the TDS / network-physiology community), and the main practical message ("rescale your parameters to your time scale") could be stated in one paragraph. I lean towards a reject at a selective venue. The paper would be a reasonable software/methods note for a specialised journal after it reframes aTDS against rescaling plus calibration and fixes the ECG delay discussion.
