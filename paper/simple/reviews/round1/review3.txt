THOUGHT:
The paper proposes "adaptive TDS" (aTDS), which sets three TDS hyper-parameters from the data. The window is 30 times the largest first-zero-truncated Bartlett factor. The lag range is half the window, capped at half a detected rhythm period. The tolerance is the largest value that keeps the chance score of circularly rotated copies, plus 2 SE, under 5 %. The motivation is real and clearly shown: TDS parameters are in samples, so they lose meaning across sampling rates and time scales. The ±30 lag-range example, where high TDS scores come with wrong, sign-flipped delays, is a useful cautionary result for the network-physiology community.

My main reservations are about how deep and how new the method is, and about some evaluation choices.

(1) Novelty. Each rule is a sensible heuristic: an effective-sample-size window, the maximal non-aliasing lag, and surrogate calibration of a threshold. The constants are conventions (30 independent samples, 5 % budget, 61-sample minimum, 0.3 rhythm threshold, 20 rotations, 50 pairs, 10 % cap). The authors themselves admit two things. Rescaling the fixed parameters by a factor estimated from the correlation time performs similarly to aTDS. An oracle with longer windows and tolerance 0 is clearly better. So the contribution is mainly "scale TDS to the time scale of the data", which is close to obvious once stated. The surrogate-calibrated tolerance is the most distinctive part, but its benefit in isolation is modest: the AUC gain is not significant, though delay recovery improves at r = 8.

(2) Theory. The Cauchy–Schwarz argument that max_i B_i bounds the pairwise inflation factor applies to the full Bartlett sums. With different truncation points per signal (first zero crossing), the bound no longer follows strictly. Also, |sum r_x r_y| ≤ max sum r_i^2 does not by itself account for negative lobes or seasonal structure, which the paper shows matter (L0123002). The "30 independent samples" interpretation is therefore approximate.

(3) Comparability of scores. aTDS uses much wider tolerances (up to ~10 % of the lag range), so raw scores are not comparable with fixed TDS, as the authors acknowledge. Some headline comparisons still rely on scores or score-based AUCs. Example: the delay patterns, where stable windows are 86–90 % vs 6–11 %, which partly reflects tolerance ±13 vs ±1. False stability also rises from <1 % to ~4 %.

(4) ECG/PPG. This is an attractive new application. However:
- Parameters are set separately for each pair, controls included. The AUC therefore compares scores obtained under different parameter sets, and the per-pair tolerance is calibrated against each pair's own rotations.
- In 12/53 true pairs, calibration failed and tolerance 0 was used, so the procedure is not fully automatic for strongly periodic signals.
- The median pulse arrival time (0.53 s) exceeds the half-beat lag cap (~0.32–0.34 s). The delay is thus only identifiable modulo a beat, and with |C| even modulo half a beat. aTDS is worse than fixed TDS against the physiological reference (PAT: 24/53 vs 32/41).
- The coupling here is essentially trivial, since a cardiac waveform is present in both signals. Detecting it shows sensitivity, but says little about the subtle couplings TDS is used for.

(5) Sleep cohort. In the design regime, aTDS gives no gain. The uncapped window (48 min) cannot resolve stages, and the wake contrast is significantly weaker. This is honestly reported, but it means the method needs an analyst-chosen cap or a stage-aware variant, which undercuts the "data-driven" claim for the main application domain of TDS.

(6) Other fields. The paper recovers 3 of 5 expected delays. In those records, tolerance calibration and the significance test use rotations of the same record. This is a mild double use, which the EEG split-half analysis avoids. Expected delays are loosely specified ("0–3 days", "about 8 h"). Some checks are against the full-record cross-correlation, which a simpler estimator already provides.

(7) Presentation. The manuscript is dense and very number-heavy, with many single-configuration experiments, and it reads more like a technical report. The scorecard (15 better / 7 equal / 4 worse) mixes tests of very different weight. Some "better" entries come from fixed TDS applied outside its intended regime, which is partly a straw man; rescaled TDS would be the fairer baseline throughout, but it appears only in the slow-down test.

Strengths: honest reporting, including negative results; train/test separation; bootstrap and equivalence testing; open code with fixed seeds; reproduction of the MATLAB benchmark; a clear, practical failure mode of fixed TDS (lag-range aliasing with wrong delays).

Overall: a useful, carefully executed engineering contribution with limited methodological novelty. Its advantage over a simple rescaling baseline is not established, and the gains in its main physiological use case (sleep networks) are null or negative. For a selective venue I lean towards rejection. It would fit a specialised methods or software journal after the changes below.
