THOUGHT:
The paper proposes "adaptive TDS" (aTDS), a set of three data-driven rules for the window length, lag range and stability tolerance of Time Delay Stability (TDS): window = 30 x max_i Bartlett factor (truncated at the first zero crossing), lag range = (L-1)/2 capped at half a detected rhythm period, and tolerance = the largest t for which the mean + 2 SE chance score of circularly rotated copies stays at or below 5 %. It evaluates each rule in isolation on AR(1) simulations, runs a slow-down test against fixed, rescaled and oracle settings, tests time-varying delays and other signal classes, and applies the method to an EEG benchmark pair, BIDMC ECG/PPG at 125 Hz, a 35-subject sleep cohort, and hydrological, energy and fuel-price records.

My overall impression is that this is a careful, honest and practically useful engineering contribution with modest conceptual novelty. Its strengths are unusual transparency (Table 7 lists losses; the sleep cohort shows no gain; failures on fuel prices and a seasonal catchment are reported), separation of training and test data in the simulations, paired bootstrap and equivalence testing, and a clear demonstration of a real failure mode of fixed TDS: a fixed lag range returns high scores with wrong, even sign-flipped, delays. That last finding is probably the most valuable and transferable result.

My main concerns:

1. Novelty and the key baseline. Scaling the window by an effective-sample-size argument (Bartlett, Bayley-Hammersley) is classical, and the lag range = L/2 rule is the same ratio fixed TDS already uses. The Limitations section concedes that "multiplying the fixed parameters by a factor estimated from the correlation time performed similarly to aTDS in the slow-down test." This simple, label-free baseline is the natural competitor, but it appears in no table or figure. The "rescaled" baseline uses the true r, which real data do not provide. Without the estimated-rescaling baseline in the main results (slow-down, delay patterns, signal classes, ECG), the added value of the specific aTDS rules over the simplest data-driven alternative is not shown. Most of the gain over fixed TDS may come simply from matching the time scale.

2. Meaning of the tolerance and comparability of scores. The calibrated tolerance grows to roughly 5-10 % of the lag range (for example ±56 samples, ±136 s, ±1.8 days). "Stable" then means something much weaker than in the original TDS. The paper acknowledges that aTDS scores cannot be compared with earlier TDS studies, nor across settings. This undermines a key appeal of TDS, an interpretable and comparable score. The "explicit false-alarm budget" is a budget on window-level false stability averaged over rotations, not a link-level error rate. The abstract's phrase "explicit false-alarm budget" risks being read as the latter. Also, mean + 2 SE over 20 rotations is a heuristic, not a coverage guarantee: at r = 16 it reduced AUC, and 4.0-4.3 % false stability, near the budget, is roughly 5x that of fixed TDS.

3. Fairness of the physiological comparison. In the ECG/PPG experiment fixed TDS is applied at 125 Hz with a 0.48 s window, which no practitioner would do. TDS studies resample to 1 Hz or use beat-to-beat series. Beating this configuration largely restates the motivation rather than validating aTDS against a realistic practice. aTDS parameters were also calibrated per pair on rotations of the very pair that was then scored, unlike the train/test split used elsewhere. Calibration failed in 12/53 pairs because the rotated heartbeat signals remain aligned with the ECG by chance, which exposes a structural weakness of the rotation null for periodic signals. Delay accuracy against the beat-by-beat pulse arrival time is worse than fixed TDS (24/53 vs 32/41).

4. "Arbitrary systems" claim is overstated. In the domain fixed TDS was designed for (1 Hz sleep), the uncapped aTDS window (48 min) cannot resolve sleep stages. An analyst-chosen cap or a stage-aware window is required, and there is no gain, with a significantly weaker wake contrast. Seasonal and rhythmic signals need a manual cap; fast signals with long delays need a user-supplied dmax; fuel prices yield no delay. So in practice aTDS still requires domain choices in many realistic cases. The abstract and conclusions ("extends TDS to arbitrary systems and sampling rates") should be toned down.

5. Theoretical grounding. Eq. (5) bounds the variance-inflation factor under the null of independent series using the full sums, but B_i is computed with sums truncated at each signal's own first zero crossing. The bound therefore does not strictly hold for the estimator used, and Bartlett's formula does not cover coupled pairs. The constant 30 is motivated by a rough extreme-value argument and is admitted to be a convention. Several new constants replace the three old ones (30, 5 %, 61, 0.1T, rhythm threshold 0.3, a = max(L, T/10), 50 pairs, 20 rotations), and sensitivity to most of them is not reported. The window rule is shown to sit below the optimum (the oracle wins at every r >= 2 by choosing much longer windows).

6. Reporting issues. The default "non-causal stability anchor" changes the benchmark score from 43.53 % to 58.1 %. This is a material implementation difference from the original TDS that is used throughout but never defined or justified. Table 7's vote count (15 better / 7 equal / 4 worse) mixes non-independent tests, includes the criterion "clear difference in counts," and counts the d = 30 rhythm case as "better" although the delay is wrong under both settings. The r = 16 paired difference (+0.14 [0.03, 0.25]) appears only in Table 7. The pair-specific joint Bartlett factor performed better in the one configuration tried; this suggests the max-B design is not the right default and deserves more evaluation.

Overall: a solid, reproducible, honestly reported methods paper whose central practical message (fixed sample-based TDS parameters can fail silently; scale them to the data) is well supported. However, the specific contribution of the aTDS rules beyond simple time-scale rescaling is not demonstrated, the comparisons on recorded physiological data use a strawman baseline, and the claims of generality are stronger than the evidence. I lean towards rejection in the current form but consider it fixable. The paper would be considerably strengthened by including the estimated-rescaling baseline everywhere and a realistic fixed-TDS pipeline (resampled / beat-to-beat) for the ECG data.
