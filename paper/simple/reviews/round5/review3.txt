THOUGHT:
The paper proposes adaptive TDS (aTDS): three data-driven rules for the window length (30 "independent samples" of the slowest signal via a truncated Bartlett factor), the lag range (half the window, capped at half a detected rhythm period) and the stability tolerance (largest tolerance for which circularly rotated copies keep the upper bound C + 2SE of the mean chance score below 5 %). The 4-of-5 stability rule is kept. Evaluation: per-rule ablations on AR(1) pairs, a slow-down test against fixed, data-rescaled, manually rescaled and oracle settings, time-varying delay patterns, a sleep-EEG benchmark pair, a 35-subject sleep cohort, BIDMC ECG/PPG at 125 Hz, and five records from hydrology, energy and fuel prices.

My first impression is that the paper is unusually honest and careful. It states plainly that none of the ingredients are new, that rescaling captures most of the gain, that the oracle beats aTDS, and that aTDS gives no gain on 1-Hz sleep networks, where TDS is mostly used. That honesty is a strength. It also makes the size of the contribution the central question, and my reasoning turns on these points:

1. Contribution relative to the obvious baseline. The core failure mode (sample-counted parameters do not transfer across time scales or sampling rates) is real but fairly self-evident. The paper's own strongest baseline, rescaled fixed TDS (one factor from the 1/e autocorrelation time), matches or beats aTDS in AUC at r = 2, 4 and 16 (0.691 vs 0.645 at r = 16) and on ECG (0.962 vs 0.963). aTDS is clearly better only at r = 8 and in the delay-found share at r = 8. So the incremental value of the per-parameter rules over a one-line rescaling rests mainly on two things: the control level in the rhythmic ECG data and the rhythm cap. That is a narrower claim than the abstract and conclusions suggest, and it is supported by one real dataset only.

2. The tolerance rule changes the meaning of "stable". Calibrated tolerances reach ±13 of ±129 and ±56 of ±541 samples. A 4-of-5 agreement within ±56 samples is a much looser notion of "a nearly constant delay" than the original ±1. The budget is set on the mean window-level chance score, so the operating point is controlled. But the scores are, as the authors admit, not comparable to prior TDS literature, and they are not comparable across systems with different time scales either. That undercuts the reproducibility motivation in the introduction: two studies using aTDS on different data get scores on different, data-dependent scales. The paper should discuss whether a normalised quantity (score minus chance, or a per-link p-value) should be the reported output.

3. Window rule justification. The Cauchy–Schwarz argument is correct for full sums under independence, and the paper correctly says that truncation at the first zero crossing breaks the bound and that coupled pairs are not covered. The constant 30 is a convention. The noise-peak calculation (0.18·sqrt(2 ln 30)) ignores that adjacent lags are correlated, but this is only illustrative. The rule fails for seasonal signals (catchment L0123002, a three-year window) and for long-memory signals (the window grows with T). In the sleep cohort it produces a 48-minute window that cannot resolve stages. So in practice users must often impose a cap, which reintroduces a manual parameter.

4. Evaluation design issues.
   - In the ECG study, parameters are medians over the same 53 patients that are scored, and the tolerance is calibrated on rotations of the same true pairs. This uses no labels, but unlike the simulations there is no train/test split.
   - The controls (ECG of one patient with the pulse of another) differ in heart rate. This makes them relatively easy negatives and partly explains why periodic controls reach high scores under rescaled TDS but not under aTDS's tolerance 0.
   - The delay reference (full-record cross-correlation peak within half a beat) shares the half-beat restriction with the aTDS lag cap. Against the physiologically meaningful pulse arrival time, fixed TDS agrees more often than aTDS. That is an important negative result for a method marketed partly on delay correctness.
   - Simulations are dominated by linear AR(1) Gaussian coupling. Nonlinear and chaotic cases are only in the supplement.
   - There is no comparison with standard windowed time-delay estimators (e.g., GCC/PHAT with a significance threshold) or with TDSpy's parameter guidance. Such a comparison would show whether the stability framework adds value over simply estimating the delay per window with a calibrated detection threshold.
5. Non-causal anchor as default. The default labelling differs from the original algorithm (58.1 % vs 43.5 % on the benchmark). This is clearly stated, but it makes "fixed TDS" in this paper a variant rather than the published method, unless the causal anchor is used.
6. Real-data results are mixed: no gain on sleep, delays recovered in 3 of 5 out-of-domain records, and the fuel-price delay is unresolved. The time-varying delay patterns are convincing, but only at strong coupling (c = 0.6) and with AUC 1.00 for both methods. A weaker-coupling version would be more informative about tracking.
7. Clarity: the writing is precise but very dense, with heavy reliance on Supplementary Sections S1–S9, and many hedges in the abstract. The abstract's "AUC 0.92 for a four-times slower system" omits that the rescaled baseline reaches 0.955 there.

Overall: a careful, transparent, reproducible engineering contribution with modest novelty and a narrow demonstrated advantage over a trivial rescaling baseline. It is useful for TDS practitioners, but the main claims of benefit beyond rescaling are thinly supported, and some design choices (a wide calibrated tolerance and data-dependent score scales) weaken the reproducibility argument. I lean toward reject at a selective venue (around 5), though it would suit a methods journal or software track after revision.
