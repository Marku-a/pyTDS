THOUGHT:
The paper proposes "adaptive TDS" (aTDS): data-driven rules for the three sample-based parameters of Time Delay Stability. The window holds 30 Bartlett-effective samples of the slowest signal. The lag range is half the window, capped at half a detected rhythm period. The tolerance is the largest one for which circularly rotated copies keep the mean chance score (+2 SE) under a 5 % "false-stability budget". The evaluation is large: per-rule ablations, a slow-down test against fixed, rescaled and oracle settings, time-varying delay patterns, several signal classes, the EEG benchmark pair, a 35-subject sleep cohort, 53 BIDMC ECG/PPG recordings at 125 Hz, and hydrological, energy and fuel-price records.

My intuitions:
- The problem is real and practically relevant. TDS parameters are expressed in samples, so they do not carry over to other sampling rates or time scales. The paper is unusually candid about its limits. It concedes no gain in the design regime (1-Hz sleep), it reports a significantly weaker wake contrast, it reports failures (fuel prices, the seasonal catchment), and it notes that a simple estimated rescaling performs about as well. Table 8 is a commendable summary.
- The novelty is modest. Each building block is standard: the Bartlett/Bayley-Hammersley effective sample size, the circular-shift surrogate null, and the Nyquist-like half-window lag limit. The contribution is engineering: assembling them into a TDS parameter recipe. That is useful to the network-physiology community but conceptually incremental.
- The central comparison is weak. Most "better" outcomes are against fixed TDS applied far outside its design regime. Beating a 60-sample window at r = 8, or at 125 Hz on ECG, is close to guaranteed. The informative baseline is rescaling by an estimated time-scale factor. The authors say it "performed similarly" but test it only in the slow-down experiment. Against the oracle-free rescaled setting (given true r), aTDS is equivalent or undecided except at r = 8. So the value added by the specific rules over naive rescaling is not established.
- The lag-range finding (delays beyond ±30 cannot be found with a ±30 search) is true by construction and hardly a result. aTDS has the same failure mode for fast signals with long delays unless the user supplies dmax (Fig. 8, d = 50: AUC 0.56 for both). The abstract's framing of "fails without any indication" as something aTDS fixes is therefore overstated.
- The rotation null breaks down for periodic signals, the very case of the main new real-data application. In 12/53 per-pair ECG calibrations no tolerance met the budget even at t = 0, because rotated heartbeat signals stay aligned by chance. In the pooled set the tolerance is 0. So on the headline real-world dataset the tolerance rule effectively collapses to a fixed value, and the gain comes from the window and lag rules. This needs to be stated explicitly.
- The rhythm-cap sensitivity result is troubling. At threshold 0.4 the rhythm is not detected, and performance is better (delay found 100 % at d = 30 vs 0 % with the cap). The cap therefore hurt in that test, which questions the default.
- Wide tolerances (±136 s in sleep, ±56 samples at r = 16, roughly 10 % of the lag range) change what "stable" means. Scores become incomparable with the literature, which the authors acknowledge. The oracle prefers long windows with tolerance 0, which suggests the tolerance rule partly compensates for a deliberately short window.
- Internal inconsistencies:
  - Section 2.2 calls the non-causal anchor "the offline definition of Bashan et al.", yet the original MATLAB score (43.61 %) is reproduced only with the causal anchor (43.53 %); the non-causal anchor gives 58.1 %. Which definition is the original?
  - The Table 8 tallies don't match the conclusions. Counting the rows outside the design regime, I get about 23 comparisons with 15 "better", not 21 and 14.
  - Eq. (6) uses (L−1)/2 but Algorithm 1 uses the floor.
  - The ECG results report 40/53 vs 38/50 delay agreement in different places.
- The ECG baseline is a straw man. The authors reject 1-Hz resampling, but they did not try a rescaled fixed TDS (e.g., 60·125 samples, or downsampling to a modest rate).
- Simulations rely heavily on linear Gaussian AR(1) coupling, which suits cross-correlation. The sleep cohort is private, which limits reproducibility of that part.

Decision: a careful and honest but incremental methods paper. The main evidence of benefit rests on mismatched baselines, and the comparison with simple rescaling is incomplete. I lean toward reject (5), but would be open to acceptance after a fuller comparison with estimated rescaling and the consistency fixes.
