THOUGHT:
The paper proposes aTDS, a set of three heuristic rules for choosing the window length (30 x max Bartlett factor), the lag range ((L-1)/2 with a half-period rhythm cap) and the stability tolerance (largest t whose rotation-null chance score plus 2 SE stays within 5 %) of Time Delay Stability. The problem is real. TDS parameters are given in samples, and the paper's lag-range example makes a good point: high TDS scores (72-96 %) can come with wrong, even sign-flipped, delays. The writing is careful and unusually candid. Limitations, failed cases (seasonal catchment, weekly fuel prices) and the lack of any gain on 1-Hz sleep data are all reported. The summary table of comparisons is useful, although it amounts to vote counting.

My main reservations:
1. Weak baseline. Most "better" results compare aTDS with fixed parameters used far outside their design scale (slowed AR processes, 125-Hz ECG). That fixed TDS fails there is close to true by construction. The natural competitor is rescaling the fixed parameters by an estimated time scale (e.g., correlation time or Bartlett factor). The authors say it "performed similarly" in the slow-down test and did not compare it anywhere else. The known-r rescaled baseline is equivalent or undecided against aTDS except at r = 8. So the gain over a simple rescaling, which is the real contribution, is not shown. The authors themselves list it only as "justification for each rule, rhythm cap, calibrated tolerance".
2. The tolerance rule changes what TDS measures. Tolerances reach about 10 % of the lag range (±13 for a delay of 20; ±136 s in the sleep cohort). Scores are then not comparable with earlier TDS values or across datasets. The false-stability budget is a mean over windows, not a link-level error rate. In 12 of 53 ECG pairs calibration failed, and these pairs were still scored at tol 0 rather than excluded or handled in a principled way. The C+2SE rule also lowers detection at r = 16 (0.65 vs 0.72).
3. Theory is thin. The constant 30 is a convention, and the √(2 ln 30) argument is heuristic. The Cauchy-Schwarz bound justifying max B_i does not hold for the truncated estimator actually used, which the authors admit. The joint factor B_xy is tested in a single configuration. Long-memory signals make L depend on record length.
4. Inconsistency in the benchmark claim. Section 2.2 calls the non-causal anchor "the offline definition of Bashan et al.", yet the original MATLAB value of 43.61 % is reproduced only with the causal anchor, while the default non-causal anchor gives 58.1 %. This should be resolved: which anchor did the original method use? The abstract's "reproduces the benchmark" refers to a non-default setting.
5. Real-data results. On the design-case sleep cohort, aTDS brings no gain and weakens the wake contrast. The uncapped window (48 min) cannot resolve stages, so a manually or stage-derived cap is required, which partly defeats the adaptive premise. The ECG result is the strongest real-data evidence. Its fixed-TDS comparator is, however, a straw man, since nobody would run 60-sample windows at 125 Hz. The one-set parameters were derived from true pairs only (medians and rotations), although this is not label leakage in a strict sense. Delay agreement with pulse arrival time is worse than fixed TDS.
6. Statistics. The 26 comparisons are not independent, and "better" mixes criteria (non-overlapping intervals, count differences). Some sample sizes are small (20 pairs per point, 10 pairs per delay).

Overall: a careful, honest engineering paper with modest novelty. Its most useful finding is the lag-range failure mode and a reproducible default procedure. Its central claim of advantage over simple rescaling is not established. I lean towards rejection for a selective venue; it would fit a methods or software journal after revision.
